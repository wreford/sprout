How Long Bad News Takes to Reach the Plan

Every problem has two dates: when someone on site first saw it, and when it showed up in the plan. Call the gap bad-news latency. On most jobs it runs to days or weeks, and for all of that time decisions are being made from a plan that is wrong.

Nobody tracks it, so nobody manages it. Measuring it is simple: log when each new constraint was first seen and when it entered the plan, and watch the median gap week to week.

Where the days go

  • Seeing it and saying it. Quiet crews don't pass things on (inside jokes), people who expect blame keep quiet (problem employees), and small injuries go unreported (minor injuries).
  • Writing it down. If capture takes more than a minute, it waits for Friday. A tap on the timesheet, a phone and a thirty-second video record it on the spot, once.
  • Getting it into the plan. The whiteboard knows by 6 a.m. The schedule finds out at the monthly update, too late for the morning question.
  • Partners. A supplier who has been given padded dates sits on their own delays (suppliers).
  • Reporting. Unsigned green status and stale baselines hide what someone already knows.

Why the delay is expensive

A week of delay on most news costs about a week. On a closing window it can cost a season. On a decision that can't be reversed, the news may arrive after the decision is made. And the forecast range can't narrow faster than new problems reach the plan.

Most fixes in these notes shorten one stage of the delay. Measuring the whole delay shows which stage is slowest on your job.

Inside Jokes Are a Good Sign

A crew that draws cartoons on the trailer whiteboard is a crew that talks. The GIF about the sticking valve in Sector 4 does more to keep people away from that valve than the line in last month's report.

Worry about the quiet site. If nobody jokes, nobody is passing on what they know either.

If the whiteboard is full of stick figures, leave it up.

Put Odd People Where They Fit

The person who annoys everyone by checking every bolt callout is the one who catches the wrong grade before the pour. The one who argues in every meeting is the one asking what happens when the crane goes down.

Most difficult people have one habit that is very valuable in the right job and tiresome in the wrong one. Don't train it out of them. Move them to where it pays.

Most Problem Employees Are Reacting to the Job

Give someone who looks checked out a ready work front, the right tools and a straight answer when they ask, and most of them start working well. A lot of bad attitudes are reasonable responses to a badly run job.

A few people do damage on purpose: they hide information, misreport progress, or take safety shortcuts with other people's lives. Remove them quickly. Everyone else is watching what you tolerate.

Match the Rigour to the Consequence

A temporary laydown pad does not need the paperwork of a pressure boundary. Spending four days perfecting it costs four days on work that drives the finish.

Decide early which work carries real consequence: life safety, pressure retention, critical tie-ins, long-lead equipment. Hold that to the highest standard. For everything else, the standard is that it works, passes inspection and doesn't hold up the next crew. Say which is which out loud, so crews don't have to guess.

Give Instructions With Tolerances

Drawings say ±5 mm. Instructions say "ASAP". The crew can't tell a hold point from a favour, so they treat everything as urgent and get worn out by work that wasn't.

Give the range you'd accept:

  • When: "Next week. A couple of days late is fine."
  • Where: "Somewhere near the break trailer that doesn't block the door."
  • What happens if it goes wrong: "If you nick the phone line it's a repair call, not an injury."

People make good calls when they know how much room they have.

Nov 26, 2026 scheduling theory

Define the Activity, Not Just Its Duration

"Hydrovac the copper lines, 3 days" invites three days of digging whether you need it or not. "Hydrovac until you can see the layout, a day at most" tells the crew when they're done.

For uncertain work, set the stopping point and the limit. The duration follows. Without a stopping point, work expands to fill the bar.

Nov 26, 2026 scheduling metrics

Check Your Float

Float is only as good as the logic under it. One missing tie and an activity shows eight days of slack it doesn't have. One padded duration and a path looks critical when it isn't.

Both errors cost you: the first gets ignored until it bites, the second gets resources it doesn't need. Before trusting a float number, walk the logic behind it with the people doing the work.

Nov 26, 2026 scheduling priorities

Not All Milestones Matter Equally

A submittal date and first energization get the same diamond on the schedule. They are not the same. Miss the freeze-up window and you lose a season. Miss an internal rough-in target by three days with the next trade not mobilized, and nothing happens.

Name the three or four milestones the job depends on. Protect those. If every date is treated as critical, crews learn that none are.

Nov 26, 2026 field trust cost

Treat Suppliers Like Part of the Team

Suppliers know when your "drop-dead" date has two weeks of padding in it. Lie to them and they stop telling you about the problems in their shop until it's too late to work around them.

Give them the actual install date and where you have slack. Call them in when a detail looks wrong, before it becomes a string of RFIs. Clear laydown before the truck arrives.

When something goes wrong, the supplier who trusts you finds a way. The one you've mistreated sends you the contract.

Nov 26, 2026 field trust

Use Your Own People Before Consultants

A consultant at $350 an hour spends the first month learning what your superintendent already knows: which permits are slow, which substation feeds what, why the north yard floods in spring. Then the report repeats what the site team said in the first interview.

Your people live with the result. Consultants are worth it for narrow, rare problems: a forensic metallurgist, a one-off regulatory witness. For running the work, invest in the people already there.

Give Everyone a Desk and a Locker

If someone keeps their tools in their truck and does paperwork on a gangbox, you've told them they're temporary. Most people act accordingly.

A shared desk, a chair, working Wi-Fi and a locker that locks are cheap. Include the cleaners, yard hands and apprentices. The people who keep the site running notice who got a space and who didn't.

Sep 13, 2026 field software

Collect Status Through the Timesheet

Status forms are optional, so they don't get filled in. Timesheets always do, because people want to be paid.

Put one question on the timesheet for each task: done, partial or blocked. Blocked shows that task's open constraints to pick from, or takes a photo of a new one. It adds a minute at the end of the shift, and the schedule is current every night.

The Two-Hour Wall

The fire code requires a two-hour wall. That requirement gets copied into the design basis, a wall type, a firestop detail, a QC record and a commissioning check. Then the project closes out and the copies scatter.

Ten years later an electrician cores a cable tray through it with no idea it's rated. Keep the requirement as one record that design, construction and operations all point to. Then the person holding the drill can find out why the wall is there.

Recurring Work Gets Lost in One Long Bar

Weekly scaffold inspections show up as one 200-day bar. The bar can't be late, so nobody notices when this week's inspection is.

The tag expires Thursday. Friday, three crews have no legal platform, and their timesheets record low productivity. Schedule each inspection as its own item with an expiry, and on Wednesday you can see whose platform is about to lapse.

Phones Cost Less Than Crew Time

A crew-hour costs more than a phone. Yet field data still gets written on paper, carried to the trailer and typed in after the crew goes home.

Company phones are allowed on almost every site, so policy isn't the obstacle. The obstacle is software that works with gloves on, in the rain, in ten seconds. The timesheet is the right place to start, because everyone opens it.

Sep 11, 2026 theory trust

Add Reversibility to Risk

Safety learned to put attention on what can kill people, not on what happens often. Project risk can borrow that.

Risk matrices score probability and severity. Add reversibility. A cost overrun spread over three years has many points where you can step in. A contract clause signed in an afternoon may have none. Where the loss is severe and can't be undone, treat it as if it will happen, and put a senior person on the decision.

Sep 11, 2026 field trust

Track Minor Injuries Too

Minor cuts and strains go unreported because reporting takes longer than the bandage. If logging one takes more than ten seconds, you'll have no data.

Once you have it, the patterns are obvious: the same knife, the same task, the same corner of the site. Small injuries don't predict fatalities, but they do repeat for the same reasons, and the fixes are usually cheap.

Crews Are More Than Headcount

The plan prices six welders at a rate. A crew that has worked together all season outperforms six welders who met on Monday, at the same rate.

That difference is in who knows whose signals, and who doesn't need to be told the sequence. Resource levelling can't see it. Splitting a good crew is a schedule decision, even though it never appears in the schedule.

Plan With Weather Data

Weather is the only project risk with a free, public, daily probabilistic forecast. Most schedules still plan a February pour like a June one.

Thirty years of records tell you how many workable pour days February usually has. Plan on that range. The ten-day forecast tells you when Thursday's wind will exceed the lift limit. Today it lives on the superintendent's phone, and the schedule finds out from the delay notice.

Sep 11, 2026 metrics theory

Count How Fast You Find New Problems

You can't list your unknown unknowns. You can count how many new constraints turn up each week.

That number should fall as the job matures. It also falls when people stop looking. So count the looking too: walkdowns, assumption checks, design reviews. If new problems fall while the checks hold steady, you're learning. If both fall, the surprises are still coming.

Track Material at Every Handoff

A pipe spool is fabricated, shipped, received, staged and installed, and each of those steps is in a different system. Finding it still takes three phone calls.

Couriers solved this decades ago with a scan at every handoff. If a crew has to ask where something is, the tracking is missing a step.

Diagrams Should Come From the Plan

A roadmap drawn by hand has boxes without dates, arrows without logic and no owners. Nobody can check it, so nobody can prove it wrong. That's why it survives.

Draw the picture from the plan. Then it changes when the plan changes, and it can't drift into a second version of the project.

Short Videos Settle Disputes

Half of all claims are arguments about what the work area looked like on a given date. Thirty seconds of video taken that day ends the argument.

The phones are already on site. Attach the clip to the item it relates to, and the evidence is filed before anyone knows they'll need it.

Make Signatures Quick and Traceable

A hold-point signature can mean a drive across site, a binder and a wet pen. So people batch them, or sign without looking, and a signature stops meaning much.

Make signing take five seconds on a phone, with the signer's name and time recorded permanently. Easy to give, impossible to deny.

Sep 11, 2026 trust field

Record Delegation With an End Date

Who can sign for whom is usually known to two people and an out-of-office reply. When the signer goes on holiday, the job finds out at the first stalled hold point.

Write delegation down: from whom, to whom, for what, until when. Then absences stop stopping work.

Sep 11, 2026 software theory

Write Procedures as Steps, Not PDFs

A procedure in a PDF is advice. The crew carries out a sequence of steps, checks, hold points and qualifications, whatever the document looks like.

Write it as structured steps and each run produces a record you can check. Change the procedure once and the next run uses the new version, instead of the one printed in somebody's binder.

Sep 11, 2026 constraints field

Which Constraint Should We Clear Today?

Status meetings ask what happened yesterday. The useful question is which blocked item, if cleared today, frees up the most work.

Constraint logs sort by date raised or by owner, never by work released. Good superintendents rank them in their heads at 5:30 a.m. Put that ranking somewhere the whole team can see it.

Sep 11, 2026 scheduling metrics

The Critical Path Changes

Run a schedule through a thousand simulations of weather and deliveries and a different path is critical in about a third of them. The printed critical path is one of those outcomes.

It also moves with every update, and crews get pulled toward whatever went red this month. A path that is critical in 60 runs out of 100 needs attention every month, whatever the printout says.

Give Assumptions a Recheck Date

Ground conditions, vendor capacity, "the permit will renew": written down at sanction and never read again. An assumption is a risk nobody has checked yet.

Assumptions go stale at different rates. Soil conditions hold for years; a vendor's shop loading changes weekly. Give each one an owner and a recheck date that matches how fast it can change.

Sep 11, 2026 scheduling metrics

Baselines Go Stale

Two winters and forty change orders after sanction, the job is still measured against the sanction-day plan, and the gap is called variance.

Rebaselining feels like admitting failure, so it happens late, and every report in between measures distance from a plan nobody believes. Keep every forecast. The baseline is the one with the contract attached. Score how accurate each forecast turned out to be, and rebaselining becomes routine.

The Whiteboard and the Schedule Drift Apart

The schedule has five thousand activities. The site runs off a whiteboard three weeks deep, updated at 6 a.m. Both are needed: the contract needs the full record, and the crew needs this week.

The trouble starts when the whiteboard becomes the working plan and never feeds back. Within a season, the schedule describes a different job. Treat them as two views of the same plan, and make changes on the board flow back.

Sep 10, 2026 constraints theory scheduling

Everything Is a Constraint

A predecessor, a missing spool, an unsigned permit, a crane booked elsewhere, wind over the lift limit, a room too crowded to work in, a dose budget nearly spent. Seven systems manage them. They're all the same thing: a condition that must be true before work can start.

CPM made logic ties the main constraint because that was what 1960s computers could handle. Ask a superintendent what stopped work this week and it is rarely a predecessor.

Dates are outputs

If everything is a constraint, the schedule is what the constraints produce, not the plan itself. Each one has the same fields: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. A permit and a weather window differ only in how they get cleared.

Keep them in one place and you can ask each morning which ones to clear to free the most work. I don't know yet whether one system can hold all seven types well. The cost of keeping them in seven is clear.

Sep 10, 2026 constraints scheduling

Watch for Closing Windows

A pour needs three dry days. The crane leaves in October. In-water work stops when the fish return. None of these are logic ties, and most schedules don't model them.

Forty days of float in front of a window that closes in forty-one days is one day of float. The software won't say so. Miss the window and the delay is a season, not a week. Put the close dates on the board and check them every morning.

Sep 10, 2026 field scheduling

Some Resources Are Specific People

The schedule says two Class B welders. The job needs Dave, the only one qualified on that alloy, and Dave is at his daughter's wedding until Tuesday.

The histogram shows welders at sixty percent all month, which is true and useless. Name people where one person is the constraint: rare qualifications, signing authority, the operator for a tandem lift. Put their calendars in the plan, and the wedding shows up in March, when it's easy to work around.

Sep 10, 2026 field software

Capture Field Notes Once, in a Usable Shape

Foremen write down what happened every day. Then the same facts get retyped into the schedule update, the timesheet, an RFI, a QC record and, years later, a claim.

Capture each item once with four fields: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. The other documents can then be generated from it. Claims teams already rebuild those four fields from diaries, years later, at legal rates.

Sep 10, 2026 cost theory

Use One Breakdown Structure

The estimate, schedule, cost report and work packages each have a breakdown structure. They match on sanction day. By month six there are four versions and a standing meeting to reconcile them.

Each group needs a different view: cost by contract, the field by area, the schedule by system. Those can all be views of one structure.

One breakdown, with every rollup calculated from it. Copies drift. References don't.

Sep 10, 2026 trust field

Put a Name on Every Green Status

Anyone can mark a status green, so reports drift optimistic. Often green just means nobody wanted the argument.

Quality solved this long ago: a weld is accepted because a named inspector stamps it. Do the same for status. A green needs a name next to it. You'll get fewer greens, and you'll be able to believe them.

Sep 10, 2026 metrics

Measure How Certain the Forecast Is

SPI and CPI measure work done against the plan. Nothing measures how well we know the finish date, which is what the owner is paying for. A job can report green every month while it gets later.

Track the range

At any status date the finish is a range, say P10 to P90. Settling a question that had spread turnover across eight months can be worth more than a record month of quantities. Plan how fast the range should narrow, then track it.

SCI (schedule certainty index) = planned range width ÷ actual range width. Above 1 is better than planned.

CCI (cost certainty index) = the same for the estimate at completion.

The worrying case is quantities moving while the range stays wide.

Scoring the ranges

Left alone, everyone reports a narrow range. So score reported ranges against what happened, the way weather forecasters are scored, and penalize overconfidence more than admitted doubt. Most big jobs already run a schedule risk analysis and then discard the distribution. Keeping it is a cheap experiment.
