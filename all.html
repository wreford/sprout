Every Date Needs a Because

"The pour is Thursday." Why Thursday? Somebody knows: the rebar inspection is booked Wednesday afternoon, and the pump truck is free Thursday. The schedule just shows a bar starting Thursday.

Without the reason, nobody can tell what moves the date. When the inspection slips, the pour sits there on Thursday looking fine until the truck shows up.

Keep the reason next to every date: starts Thursday because the inspection lands Wednesday. Then a date can be checked, and when a reason changes, the dates that depend on it change too. It's the same idea as following the chain to a name, pointed forward.

Lags Hide Reasons

A five-day lag between two activities is usually one of three things: concrete curing, a permit or inspection, or a guess somebody made two years ago. The schedule can't tell you which.

That matters, because they behave differently. Curing is physics and won't shrink. An inspection can be booked earlier. A guess should be checked. All three look like the same grey gap.

Replace lags with the thing they stand for: "7 days of curing", "waiting on the city inspector". Then you know which ones you can do something about.

Show What Changed in the Plan

Each monthly update arrives as a new set of PDFs. To find out what changed, someone lays last month's beside this month's and squints. Most people don't, so changes slip through: a finish moved a week, a tie got deleted, a duration got shorter for no reason.

Software developers solved this long ago. Every change shows up as a short list: what was added, what was removed, what moved and by how much, and who did it.

Ask for that list with every update. Ten lines that say what changed and why are worth more than four hundred pages that say what the plan is now.

Keep Two Weeks of Ready Work

Work is ready when everything it needs is true at once: drawings issued, material on site, permit signed, area handed over, the work before it finished. One missing piece and it isn't ready, however green it looks on the schedule.

Count ready work in crew-days, area by area. If a crew has less than about two weeks of ready work ahead of it, it's going to stand around soon, and the schedule won't warn you.

The count is the early warning. It moves before productivity drops, which makes it the number to work on each morning.

Clear Holds in Batches

Five hold points need the same inspector this week. They get booked as five separate visits, because five foremen each called on their own.

Group open holds by who clears them: the inspector, the engineer of record, the owner's rep, the utility. Then one visit clears five, and the person clearing them sees the whole list instead of being interrupted all week.

It also shows who the job is waiting on most. That person deserves a standing slot, not a phone call.

Everything on Site Expires

Permits, scaffold tags, crane certifications, welder qualifications, medicals, insurance certificates, the concrete supplier's mix approval. Almost every condition for work has an end date, and the plan knows none of them.

So they lapse on a Friday and the crew finds out on Monday. Record the expiry with each one, and check the plan against it: any activity that runs past an expiry gets flagged while there's still time to renew. It's the scaffold tag problem, everywhere.

Ask What-If Before the Meeting Ends

"What happens if the crane is two weeks late?" On most jobs the answer is "let us look at it and get back to you", and the answer comes the following week, after the decision was already made by default.

If the plan is built from the conditions behind it, a what-if is a quick question: change one condition, see which dates move. The scheduler can answer while everyone's still in the room.

Fast answers change the meeting. People ask more questions and test ideas before they commit, the same way pricing a risk in days ends the argument about its colour.

Merge the Contractors' Plans Before the Site Does

The civil contractor plans to have the north road closed for trenching next week. The steel contractor plans deliveries down the north road next week. Both schedules are fine on their own.

Each contractor's plan only describes its own work. The clashes are in the shared things: roads, laydown, cranes, rooms, outages. The site finds them at 7 a.m.

Put every contractor's next three weeks against the same shared resources once a week, and look for two claims on the same thing at the same time. It's a short meeting, and it saves a lot of shouting.

Some Plans Should Be Impossible to Publish

A schedule can put two crews in one confined space, skip a hold point, or plan a lift over an energized line, and the software will print it in colour.

Some rules aren't preferences. Write those as checks the plan has to pass before it goes out, the way a building permit gets checked against the code. A plan that breaks one gets sent back, not published with a note. See writing the site rules once.

Put the Owner's Decisions on the Schedule

Owner decisions hold up work as surely as missing steel: finishes selections, a change approval, a tie-in date, a design question. They rarely appear on the schedule, so they never look late.

Put each one in as an activity with a need-by date, worked back from the work it holds up. Then the owner sees the same thing you do: decide by the 12th, or the drywall crew stops on the 20th.

It turns "waiting on the owner" from a complaint into a date, and owners respond well to dates.

Panama: Build the Railroad Before You Dig

France started the Panama Canal in 1881 and gave up after years of work, with thousands of workers dead from yellow fever and malaria and a sea-level design the ground wouldn't allow.

The United States took over in 1904. Its chief engineer from 1905, John Stevens, slowed the digging down. He rebuilt the railroad, put up housing, built water and sewer systems, and backed William Gorgas's campaign against the mosquitoes. The plan also switched to locks.

The canal opened in 1914. The lesson still holds: the logistics, health and living conditions of the workforce are the first critical path, not overhead.

The Empire State Building Was a Logistics Job

The Empire State Building went up in about thirteen months in 1930 and 1931, with the steel frame rising several floors a week. It finished early and under budget.

The speed came from logistics more than heroics. Deliveries were scheduled to the hour because there was nowhere to store material on a Manhattan block. Small rail carts on each floor moved material from the hoists to where it was needed. The design was repetitive so crews could learn it.

A tight site with no laydown forced the discipline that a roomy yard lets most jobs avoid.

Sydney Opera House: Building Before the Design Was Done

The Sydney Opera House was meant to take four years. It took fourteen, opening in 1973, at many times the original estimate.

Construction started in 1959, before anyone knew how to build the shell roofs. The podium went up first, and when the roof design was finally worked out, the podium had to be partly reworked to carry it. The architect, Jørn Utzon, left the project in 1966 and never saw it finished.

Starting early to show progress is tempting on every big job. The bill arrives later, as rework on the parts built for a design that didn't exist yet. See counting drawing revisions.

Hoover Dam Couldn't Wait for the Concrete to Cool

Concrete gives off heat as it cures. Poured as one solid mass, the Hoover Dam would have taken over a century to cool, and cracked as it did.

So the builders placed it in separate blocks and ran cold water through steel pipes embedded in the concrete, then grouted the pipes and the joints once it had cooled. The dam was finished in 1936, ahead of schedule.

A hard physical constraint can't be scheduled away. It can be redesigned around, if someone looks at it early enough.

Brooklyn Bridge: Bad Wire, Enough Margin

Partway through spinning the cables of the Brooklyn Bridge, the engineers found that a supplier had been delivering wire that had failed inspection, switched back into the deliveries after it was rejected. Much of it was already in the cables.

Pulling it out wasn't practical. Washington Roebling, the chief engineer, had designed the cables with a large safety margin, so he added extra wire to make up the strength and kept going. The bridge opened in 1883 and still carries traffic.

Roebling ran much of the job from a window, crippled by decompression sickness from the caissons. His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, carried his instructions to the site and became an expert in her own right. Margin and good people covered for a fraud that inspection missed.

Quebec Bridge: The Warning Arrived Too Late

In 1907 the Quebec Bridge collapsed during construction and killed 75 workers. Days before, the inspector on site had reported that steel members were bending.

The consulting engineer was in New York and rarely visited. The span had been lengthened without the dead load being properly recalculated. The worry went by letter and telegram, and the order to stop adding load didn't reach the site before the bridge came down.

It is the worst case of bad-news latency: the people who could see the problem couldn't stop the work, and the person who could stop it couldn't see. Canadian engineers wear an iron ring, and the story most often told about it starts here.

Paid by the Mile

The two companies building the first transcontinental railroad in the 1860s were paid in government bonds and land for each mile of track they laid. So both raced, and quality came second.

With no agreed meeting point, their grading crews passed each other and kept going, building parallel grades side by side for many miles, because every mile paid. Congress finally fixed the meeting point at Promontory, Utah, in 1869.

Contracts get what they measure. Pay for miles and you get miles, whether you needed them or not.

Crossrail: The Tunnels Were the Easy Part

London's Crossrail, now the Elizabeth line, finished its tunnels on time. The line was due to open in December 2018. Months before, it announced a delay, and it opened in May 2022, billions over budget.

The hard part was making the trains, signalling systems and stations work together. Testing that integration needed the stations finished, and the stations were late. The schedule had treated it as a final stage of testing instead of a large piece of work in its own right.

It's the last 5 percent at national scale. Visible progress on the civil works made the job look nearly done long after it wasn't.

The Big Dig and One Wrong Glue

Boston's Big Dig buried an elevated highway in tunnels. It finished in 2007, years late and billions over the early estimates.

In 2006 concrete ceiling panels in one of the tunnels fell and killed a driver. The anchors holding them were set in an epoxy that wasn't suited to holding a constant load, and it crept slowly until the anchors pulled out.

On a job that size, one product choice in one detail killed someone years after it was installed. The requirement that the anchor carry a permanent load existed. It didn't make it to the person choosing the glue.

The Channel Tunnel Met in the Middle

In 1990 the British and French tunnelling crews broke through to each other under the Channel, having started from opposite coasts many kilometres apart. The two bores met within a fraction of a metre.

That came from relentless survey control: checking position against fixed references over and over, and correcting small drifts before they became large ones. The project overran its budget badly, but the tunnels lined up.

Most project controls work the same way. A small, frequent check against a fixed reference beats a big audit at the end.

Measure Time to Tools

The shift starts at 7:00. The first tool touches the work at 7:50. In between: the toolbox talk, a permit that isn't signed yet, a hunt for the right fittings, a wait for the hoist.

Fifty minutes across a crew of twelve is ten hours a day. That's more than a full person-week lost every week, and no report shows it, because everyone was on site and busy.

Walk one crew for one morning with a watch. Note when tools first touch work and what they waited on. Most of it can be fixed the afternoon before: permit signed, material staged, hoist booked. The foreman's last hour sets up the crew's first one, so protect it.

A Change Costs More in the Field Than on Paper

A change order prices the added work: material, hours, markup. It rarely prices what the change does to everything around it. Crews get pulled off sequence, finished work gets torn out, and the same room gets visited three times.

That knock-on cost is real, can be bigger than the change itself, and turns up months later as "low productivity" with no cause attached.

When a change is issued, write down which crews move, what gets re-sequenced and what gets redone. Price it if you can. If you can't, the record is still the difference between a claim and a complaint.

Overtime Borrows From Next Month

One Saturday recovers a day. Six weeks of Saturdays doesn't recover six days. People get tired, make more mistakes, get hurt more, and start missing Mondays. Hours go up and output per hour goes down.

Use overtime like a sprint: short, aimed at one window, with an end date everyone knows. If the plan needs it for months, the plan is wrong, and the overtime is hiding that.

While it's on, track output per hour, not hours worked. When output per hour drops, the extra hours have stopped helping.

Read the Laydown Yard

Walk the yard before you read the reports. Material that showed up months early is cash already spent, sitting in the rain, getting lost or damaged. Empty racks for next week's work mean a crew is about to stand around.

The yard is where the procurement schedule meets the field's. Count days of material on hand for each work front. Too many and you're paying for a warehouse. Too few and one late truck stops a crew. Either way it should be tracked, not found.

Count Drawing Revisions

"Design is 90 percent complete" can stay true for a year. A better signal is how many drawings got revised this month, and how many of those were already issued for construction.

Every revision to an issued drawing means rework, RFIs or fabrication that no longer fits. Plot revisions per week by area. An area whose drawings are still moving isn't ready to build, whatever the percent says.

Start fabrication and field work where the drawings have stopped changing.

The First Floor Is the Slowest

Repetitive work speeds up. The tenth floor, the fortieth pipe support and the hundredth panel all go faster than the first, as the crew learns the details, the sequence and each other.

Most schedules use one average rate. So the early floors run late and look like a crisis, and the later floors finish early and hide float.

Plan the curve: slower first units, faster later ones. Don't judge the job on its first month of production. And once a crew has learned the work, keep it together.

More People Can Make It Slower

When a job falls behind, the reflex is to add crews. Put four trades in one room and they wait on each other, block each other's access and damage each other's work. Output per person drops, sometimes far enough that the room finishes later.

Before adding people, look at the space. How many crews can work in that area without waiting? Room is shared capacity, like a crane. The faster plan is often fewer crews, in sequence, each with its own area.

Look Further Ahead Than Your Longest Lead Time

A three-week lookahead can't catch a constraint that takes four weeks to clear. Permits, engineered supports, crane bookings, outage windows: if clearing one takes longer than the window, it's already late by the time anyone sees it.

Set the window by the slowest constraint you routinely clear, not by habit. Or run two: three weeks for the crew, and a longer one that only tracks the long-lead items and the closing windows.

Stop Typing Percent Complete

On most status reports, percent complete is a number somebody types. It creeps up a few points a week, because nobody wants the argument, then parks at 95 for three months.

Let the records set it. A wall is done when the inspection is signed, the deficiencies are closed and the test record is filed. A system is done when all its walls, pipes and panels are. Nobody gets to type a number.

  • The argument changes. "It's basically done" turns into "which signature is missing?"
  • Partial progress becomes a count: evidence on file against evidence required.
  • Closeout shows up in month three instead of month thirty, as missing paperwork on work everyone called finished.
  • Rollups stop lying. A floor is done when its rooms are (one breakdown).

Expect the first report built this way to show less progress than last week's typed one. That gap was always there.

Ask "Waiting on What?" Until You Hit a Name

Ask why a task isn't done and it takes three phone calls to find out. The crew is waiting on scaffold. Scaffold is waiting on a permit. The permit is waiting on a signature from someone at a cottage with no signal.

Each person on that chain knows their link. Nobody has the whole chain written down.

So write it down. For every open item, record what it's waiting on, then what that's waiting on, until you get to a person and a date. "Them" doesn't count. When five chains end at the same person, you know whose desk to visit first (the morning question).

In the next coordination meeting, take one red item and keep asking "waiting on what?" Most meetings stop at the first answer. The useful one is usually the fourth.

Price Risks in Days, Not Colours

A heat map puts a risk in a red square. The square doesn't say what the risk does to the finish date, so the meeting argues about whether it's red or orange.

Run the schedule a few thousand times with realistic spread on durations and deliveries, once with the risk and once without. The difference in the finish is what the risk costs in days. Multiply by your daily cost of delay and you have dollars.

  • The list re-sorts itself. A likely risk on a path with lots of float can cost nothing. An unlikely one on a path that is often critical can cost weeks.
  • Timing shows up. The same two-week slip costs a lot more just before a window closes.
  • Mitigations get a price too. If it costs less than the days it buys back, do it.

The number doesn't replace judgment on losses you can't undo (reversibility). Most big jobs already run this analysis once, for the owner, and file it. Run it monthly and sort the register by it.

The Crane Is a Predecessor

Two lifts that need the same crane can't happen at the same time. That's as hard a rule as rebar before concrete. Most schedules keep logic in one place and resources in another, and nobody reconciles them.

So both lifts show on Tuesday, and the crane operator picks which one waits.

Draw shared capacity as a link: these two need the same crane, the same welder, the same room, the same outage. Then the sequence on paper is the sequence the site will run. It also explains why the logic shows eight days of float that never shows up on site. The logic had eight days. The crane didn't.

On the next three-week lookahead, list every crane, hoist, specialist and shutdown, and draw a line between activities that share one. The clashes jump out.

The Schedule Doesn't Know the Site Rules

No hot work within 10 metres of fuel. Two people for a confined space entry. Hold for the inspector before backfill. Those rules live in a binder, a toolbox talk and the superintendent's head. The schedule knows none of them.

So a lookahead can put welding next to a fuel delivery, and it's down to whoever happens to notice.

Write each rule as a check the plan can run: which activities it applies to, and what has to be true first. Run the checks whenever the plan changes, and the welding-beside-fuel clash shows up on Monday's lookahead instead of on Thursday morning. The rule no longer depends on who's on shift.

Start with the five rules behind your last few near misses, and the hold points that need a signature. Check next week's lookahead against them by hand. Count how many clashes you find.

The Last 5 Percent Takes a Third of the Job

Punch lists, commissioning, turnover documents and closeout routinely eat a third of the calendar. They're planned as the last 5 percent, and nobody manages them until the crews are leaving.

By then the people who knew the systems are on another job, the records are scattered, and every open item depends on someone who has already demobilized.

  • Open a turnover package for each system on day one, with an owner, and fill it as the work gets done.
  • Close deficiencies as they come up, each with a photo or short clip, instead of finding 1,400 on the final walk.
  • Schedule commissioning as real work with durations and logic, not a diamond at the end.
  • Don't call 95 percent nearly done. The slowest third of the job hides behind it.

Pull the as-built schedules from your last few jobs and see how long closeout took. The owner's final opinion of you gets formed in this stretch, and so does the release of your retention.

Your Old Schedules Are Your Best Estimating Data

At closeout, the as-built dates for every activity go into the archive and nobody opens them again. They're the best estimating data a contractor has: its own crews, its own jobs, its own weather.

The next estimate starts from published rates and somebody's memory instead, which is why the same activities come in light on job after job.

  • Quantities with the dates. Planned and actual duration plus the quantity installed gives you a rate per crew-day.
  • Why the big misses happened. Weather, a blocked work front, rework. A slow activity with a known cause tells you something different from one nobody can explain.
  • Who did it. Crews that stayed together went faster, and some work hung on one person.

Jobs only compare if they share a breakdown structure. Do that, and estimates start from what your crews did last time, and ranges come from real spread instead of a guessed plus-or-minus.

What It Costs to Ask a Question

Every site has a price for asking a question. Some of it is time: finding who knows, then waiting for them. Some of it is looking like you should already know. When the price is high, people guess, and some of the guesses get poured in concrete.

What drives the price up

  • Nobody obvious to ask. The requirement is in a document the electrician will never see, or the one person who can decide is away and nobody else can sign (delegation).
  • Asking gets you blamed. People stop asking where questions get treated as ignorance. The person who asks awkward ones in meetings gets labelled difficult.
  • Answers come too late. A three-week RFI turnaround teaches crews to stop writing RFIs.

Bring it down

Track how many questions get asked and how long the answers take. A lot of questions is fine. Slow answers are the problem. Give instructions with tolerances so fewer questions are needed, and answer first the ones holding up the most work.

Count the Handoffs

Schedules count tasks. Most of what goes wrong happens between them, when work, information or authority passes from one crew, company or system to the next.

Each pass can drop something:

  • A requirement. The fire rating is on the drawings and missing from what the electrician sees ten years later (the two-hour wall).
  • A thing. A pipe spool that changes hands six times can vanish at any of them (material).
  • Authority. A signer on holiday stops the next crew.
  • Intent. "ASAP" arrives without the slack behind it.
  • Time. Every pass adds days before bad news reaches the plan.

Count handoffs per work package the way you count activities. Cut the ones that only exist because the same data lives in two places. For the rest, make the pass visible: a scan, a named signature, steps that travel with the work. Suppliers are the handoff you control least, so start there.

How Long Bad News Takes to Reach the Plan

Every problem has two dates: the day someone on site first saw it, and the day it showed up in the plan. On most jobs the gap is days or weeks, and the whole time, decisions get made off a plan that's wrong.

Nobody tracks the gap, so nobody manages it. Start logging both dates for each new constraint and watch the median week to week.

Where the days go

  • Saying it. Crews that don't talk and people who expect blame keep things to themselves.
  • Writing it down. If recording it takes more than a minute, it waits for Friday. A short video on a phone takes thirty seconds.
  • Getting it into the schedule. The whiteboard knows by 6 a.m. The schedule finds out at the monthly update.
  • Reporting. An unsigned green hides what somebody already knows.

A week of lag usually costs about a week. In front of a closing window it can cost a season, and on a decision you can't undo, the news shows up after you've made it.

Worry About the Quiet Site

A crew that draws cartoons on the trailer whiteboard is a crew that talks. The stick figure getting launched off the sticking valve in Sector 4 keeps more people away from that valve than last month's report did.

The site to worry about is the one where nobody jokes. People who don't kid each other usually don't tell each other much else either.

Leave the stick figures up.

Every Site Needs a Pain in the Neck

The person who drives everyone nuts checking every bolt callout is the one who catches the wrong grade before the pour. The one who argues in every meeting is the one asking what happens when the crane goes down.

Most difficult people have one habit that's worth a lot in the right seat and grating in the wrong one. Don't train it out of them. Move them to where it pays.

Most Bad Attitudes Are a Badly Run Job

Give a checked-out worker a ready work front, the right tools and a straight answer when they ask, and most of them turn around. A lot of bad attitude is a reasonable response to standing around waiting on material for the third day running.

A few people do damage on purpose. They hide problems, fudge progress, or cut safety corners with someone else's life. Get rid of them fast. Everyone else is watching what you put up with.

Not Everything Is a Nuclear Valve

A temporary laydown pad does not need the paperwork of a pressure boundary. Four days spent perfecting it are four days taken from work that drives the finish.

Decide early which work carries real consequence: life safety, pressure retention, critical tie-ins, long-lead equipment. Hold that to the top standard. For the rest, it has to work, pass inspection and not hold up the next crew. Tell people which is which, out loud, so they don't have to guess.

ASAP Is Not a Tolerance

Drawings say ±5 mm. Instructions say "ASAP". The crew can't tell a hold point from a favour, so everything gets treated as urgent, and people burn out on work that could have waited.

Give the range you'd accept:

  • When: "Next week. A couple of days late is fine."
  • Where: "Anywhere near the break trailer, as long as it doesn't block the door."
  • What if it goes wrong: "If you nick the phone line, that's a repair call. Nobody gets hurt."

People make good calls when they know how much room they have.

Everything Is a Constraint

A predecessor, a missing spool, an unsigned permit, a crane booked elsewhere, wind over the lift limit, a room too crowded to work in, a dose budget nearly spent. Seven systems manage them. They're all the same thing: something that has to be true before work can start.

CPM made logic ties the main constraint because that's what 1960s computers could handle. Ask a superintendent what stopped work this week and it's rarely a predecessor.

Dates come out the other end

If everything is a constraint, the schedule is what the constraints produce. Each one has the same fields: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. A permit and a weather window differ only in how they get cleared.

Keep them in one place and you can ask each morning which ones to clear to free the most work. I don't know yet whether one system can hold all seven kinds well. I do know what it costs to keep them in seven.

Sep 10, 2026

How Sure Are We About the Finish?

SPI and CPI measure work done against the plan. Nothing measures how well we know the finish date, and that's what the owner is paying for. A job can report green every month while it gets later.

Track the range

At any status date the finish is a range, say P10 to P90. Settling one question that had turnover spread across eight months can be worth more than a record month of quantities. Plan how fast the range should narrow, then track it.

SCI (schedule certainty index) = planned range width ÷ actual range width. Above 1 is better than planned.

CCI (cost certainty index) = the same for the estimate at completion.

The bad sign is quantities moving while the range stays wide.

Score the ranges

Left alone, everyone reports a tight range. So score reported ranges against what happened, the way weather forecasters get scored, and penalize overconfidence harder than admitted doubt. Most big jobs already run a schedule risk analysis and throw away the distribution. Keeping it costs almost nothing.
