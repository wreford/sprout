Inside Jokes Are a Good Sign

A trailer whiteboard covered in cartoons, a Teams channel full of oddly specific GIFs, jokes nobody outside the gate understands: none of that means the crew is slacking. It usually means they talk to each other.

The worrying site is the quiet one. When nobody jokes, people also stop sharing what they know. They do their hours, cover themselves and keep their heads down.

  • A GIF about the sticking gate valve in Sector 4 reminds everyone about that valve better than a line in a status report does.
  • Being able to laugh at a bad week together lowers the pressure and keeps people talking.
  • You can't create this with a poster. It comes from working on something hard together.

If the whiteboard is full of stick figures and the group chat is funny, leave it alone.

Put Odd People Where They Fit

Organizations try to make everyone polite and predictable. On a job site, the people who don't fit that mould often catch the problems everyone else misses.

  • The person obsessed with detail catches the wrong bolt callout or the permit condition before the pour.
  • The one who argues in every meeting asks what happens when the crane goes down, before the plan is approved.
  • The grumpy veteran has seen this plan fail before. Listen past the tone.
  • The one who cuts corners on paperwork usually finds the steps nobody needs.

Don't try to change them. Give each one the work where their habit helps.

Most Problem Employees Are Reacting to the Job

Most people who look lazy or checked out are reacting to a bad setup. Give them a clear work front, the right tools, real information and a boss who listens, and most of them start working well again.

A small number are different. They hide information so others fail, misreport field conditions, play politics, cut safety corners or treat people badly.

  • Your good people notice who you tolerate, and they leave if it goes on.
  • Coaching fixes skills and confidence. It doesn't fix dishonesty.
  • Removing that person quickly tells everyone the standard is real.

Be patient with almost everyone. Be quick with the few who are doing damage on purpose.

Match the Rigour to the Consequence

A laydown pad, a temporary road or a utility trench doesn't need the treatment you'd give a reactor component. Spending four days perfecting a detail that doesn't affect the milestone is four days not spent on something that does.

  • Keep the strictest standards for pressure boundaries, life safety, critical tie-ins and expensive long-lead equipment.
  • For the rest, the bar is: it holds, it passes inspection, and the sequence keeps moving.
  • Time spent polishing non-critical work comes out of time for real constraints.

Decide up front which work needs nuclear-grade care, and say so.

Give Instructions With Tolerances

"I need this ASAP" or "stage the gear over there" often means "sometime next week is fine" and "anywhere that doesn't block the trailer". Saying the second version saves people from guessing.

Drawings always carry tolerances. Task instructions almost never do, so a rough staging request sounds as urgent as a hold point.

  • Time: "Sometime next week. Earlier is good, a couple of days late is fine."
  • Place and method: "Near the break trailer, wherever it won't block access."
  • Consequence: "Hydrovac for a day around the copper phone lines. If we nick one it's a repair call, not an injury."

When you know little, the range is wide. It narrows as drawings firm up and locates come back. Tell people where it is now.

Nov 26, 2026 scheduling theory

Define the Activity, Not Just Its Duration

Typing "3 to 5 days" into a schedule bar doesn't fix a vague activity. What helps is saying how far the work goes and when to stop.

  • Effort: "Hydrovac for up to a day. Stop once you can see the layout."
  • Location: "Anywhere around the break trailer that keeps the door and lane clear."
  • Timing: "Next week, when that area is open and it doesn't clash with other work."

The duration follows from that. The hard part is setting the stopping point so the crew doesn't keep going past the point of usefulness.

Check Your Float

Scheduling software reports float as a precise number. On site it is often wrong, because of a bad logic tie, a crew moved elsewhere, a slow permit, or an activity that is less certain than its bar suggests.

  • Float that is too high hides risk. Everyone ignores the activity until the cushion is gone.
  • Float that is too low creates false alarms, and people chase the wrong problems.
  • Making float accurate means reviewing the logic, separating real constraints from assumed ones, and checking where slack exists in the work.

Wrong float is worse than none. It is worth the time to correct it.

Not All Milestones Matter Equally

Every milestone gets the same diamond in the schedule, whether it's a submittal date or energizing switchgear. If every one is treated as urgent, crews stop believing any of them are.

  • Some milestones open work fronts, release major equipment or cross safety boundaries. Others exist for a report or an invoice.
  • Missing a freeze-up window or a utility outage can cost months. Missing an internal target by three days, when the next trade hasn't arrived, may cost nothing.
  • Know the few milestones the job depends on, protect those, and be relaxed about the rest.

Tell the crew which ones are which.

Treat Suppliers Like Part of the Team

If you hide the real schedule from suppliers, shout when a delivery slips and only call when you need a miracle, you end up low on their priority list. They hold much of your critical path in their shops and yards.

  • Give them the real install date and where you have room. Padded "drop-dead" dates are obvious, and they'll stop telling you about their own problems.
  • Bring them in early when a detail is wrong or a dimension changes. It is faster than a string of RFIs.
  • Don't leave trucks waiting at the gate because nobody cleared laydown, and don't send last-minute orders you could have placed weeks ago.

Suppliers who trust you help when something goes wrong. Suppliers you mistreat do exactly what the contract says and nothing more.

Use Your Own People Before Consultants

A $350-an-hour consultant often ends up telling management what the site team already knew. By the time they learn the permits, the equipment and the site's quirks, your people could have fixed the problem.

  • Your staff already know how the site works and who makes decisions.
  • They will still be there next month and live with the result.
  • Paying an outsider to repeat their ideas tells them their judgment doesn't count.

Specialists still make sense for rare, narrow problems, like a forensic metallurgist or a one-time regulatory witness. For running the work, invest in the people you have.

Give Everyone a Desk and a Locker

Keeping gear in a car, working on a laptop in a hallway or filling in forms on a gangbox tells people they're temporary. A desk and a locker, even shared ones, are basic tools.

  • A shared desk with a monitor, a chair and working Wi-Fi is enough. So is a locker that locks.
  • Include everyone: cleaners, yard hands, material coordinators, apprentices.
  • People who have a place for their boots, notebook and lunch act like they belong on the job.

It doesn't need to be fancy. It needs to exist for everyone.

Collect Status Through the Timesheet

Optional forms lose to a busy foreman. The timesheet is the one form that always gets filled in, because people want to be paid.

So ask for task status there. To log hours against a task, the worker answers one question about it.

Keep it to a tap: done, partial or blocked. Partial asks roughly how much. Blocked shows the task's open constraints to pick from, or lets you add a new one with a photo. The options come from the plan, so each answer updates it.

It takes a few minutes before quitting time, and the schedule is current every night.

The Two-Hour Wall

The fire code says a wall must stop fire for two hours. That requirement becomes a line in the design basis, a wall type on a drawing, a firestop detail, a QC record and a commissioning check. Then the project ends, and for decades people drill holes through the wall.

Each phase copies the requirement into its own documents. Ten years later, the person cutting a cable tray through it has no idea the wall is rated.

Keep it as one record that design, construction, inspection and maintenance all point to. Then the electrician can find out why not to drill there, and the regulator can ask which walls a clause covers and when they were last checked. Today both need a records search.

Recurring Work Gets Lost in One Long Bar

Weekly scaffold inspections, permit renewals and dewatering checks usually appear as one 200-day bar.

The bar loads the resource histogram and collects cost. It can't show that this week's inspection was missed.

When a scaffold tag expires on Thursday, three crews lose their platform on Friday and their timesheets show low productivity. Schedule each occurrence as its own item with an expiry, and on Wednesday you can see which tag lapses next and who it affects.

Phones Cost Less Than Crew Time

An hour of crew time costs more than a phone, yet field data still gets carried to the trailer and typed in after the crew goes home.

"No phones on site" rules are about personal phones. Company devices are already allowed almost everywhere. The remaining problem is software.

Open questions: what does data capture look like with gloves on in the rain, who pays for the devices, and what has to be on the screen for a foreman to use it? The timesheet is one good starting point.

Sep 11, 2026 theory trust

Add Reversibility to Risk

In safety, STKY (stuff that kills you) means putting attention on what causes serious injury and death, not on minor incidents. The same idea can apply to project risk: what could kill the project?

Risk matrices have two axes, probability and severity. A useful third is reversibility. A loss that builds over three years has many points where you can step in. The same loss can also become permanent in an afternoon with one signature.

Where a risk is both severe and irreversible, likelihood matters less. Those are the decisions where a qualified person needs to be present, much like critical-step identification in nuclear operations. Small problems still matter, but they shouldn't take the attention those decisions need.

Track Minor Injuries Too

Serious hazards come first. Minor cuts and strains still matter.

Reporting a small injury usually takes longer than treating it, so it doesn't get reported. If logging takes more than ten seconds, the data won't exist.

Once it's recorded, look for patterns: cuts by knife type, strains by area, week over week. Give the most common type an owner and a change in controls. Minor injuries don't predict fatalities, but they do repeat for the same reasons.

Crews Are More Than Headcount

The plan prices labour at a rate. A crew that has worked together all season outperforms one assembled on Monday, at the same rate.

The difference comes from the crew: the operator who knows the rigger's signals, the finisher who doesn't need directions. The schedule doesn't record it, so resource levelling treats six welders as interchangeable.

Splitting a good crew is a schedule decision that doesn't show up in the schedule. Record which people work best together.

Plan With Weather Data

Weather is the one project risk with a free, daily, probabilistic forecast, yet plans often treat a January pour like a July one with a note attached.

Two free sources are usually ignored. Thirty years of records show how many workable pour days February really has, so plan on the range, not the average. The ten-day forecast is a live input too: when Thursday's wind passes the lift limit, the lift window has moved.

At the moment the forecast lives on the superintendent's phone, and the schedule finds out from the delay notice.

Sep 11, 2026 metrics theory

Count How Fast You Find New Problems

You can't list unknown unknowns, but you can count new constraints found each week.

That rate should fall as the job goes on. A falling rate can also mean people stopped looking. So count the looking as well: walkdowns, assumption checks, questions raised. New problems per check is the useful number.

If new problems fall while checks stay steady, uncertainty is shrinking, and the forecast range should narrow. If both fall, nobody is looking, and the surprises are still coming.

Track Material at Every Handoff

Six systems know about a pipe spool: fabricated, shipped, cleared, received, staged, installed. Answering "where is it?" still takes three phone calls.

Couriers solved this decades ago with a barcode scanned at every handoff. When a crew has to ask where material is, the tracking is missing a step.

Diagrams Should Come From the Plan

Roadmap diagrams often have boxes without dates, arrows without logic and no owners. Nobody can check them against reality, which is part of why they're popular.

A picture that can't be checked doesn't help run the job. Generate diagrams from the plan, so they show the same data as the plan instead of a second version of it.

Short Videos Settle Disputes

Most claims and RFIs are arguments about what the work area looked like on a certain date. A thirty-second video taken at the time usually answers that.

Every phone on site has a camera. Attach the clip to the constraint or record it relates to, and the evidence for any later dispute is collected as the job runs.

Make Signatures Quick and Traceable

A hold-point signature often means a drive, a binder and wet ink. That effort pushes people to batch signatures or skip them, which makes each one mean less.

Make signing take a few seconds on a phone, with the signer's identity recorded permanently. Easy to give, and clearly attributable.

Record Delegation With an End Date

Who can sign for whom is usually known only to a few people and their out-of-office replies. When someone goes on leave, the job finds out who can't sign as each hold point stalls.

Record delegation explicitly: from whom, to whom, for what scope, until when. Then a vacation no longer stops work.

Sep 11, 2026 software theory

Write Procedures as Steps, Not PDFs

A procedure in a PDF is guidance. What crews follow on site is a sequence of steps, conditions, hold points and qualifications.

Written as structured steps, each run creates trackable items that can be versioned, compared and audited. Change the procedure once and every future run uses the new version.

Which Constraint Should We Clear Today?

Status meetings ask what happened. The more useful question is which constraints, if cleared today, would free up the most work.

Few site tools rank constraints that way. Lists sort by date raised or by owner, not by work released. Good superintendents do this ranking in their heads early in the morning. A shared tool could do the same thing for everyone.

Sep 11, 2026 scheduling metrics

The Critical Path Changes

Run the same schedule through a thousand simulations of weather and deliveries, and a different path is critical in about a third of them. The critical path on the printout is one outcome.

It also changes with each update, so crews get pulled toward whatever turned red this month. A path that is critical in sixty out of a hundred runs deserves attention every month, whatever the printout shows.

Float describes one schedule run. Criticality across many runs describes the job.

Give Assumptions a Recheck Date

Ground conditions, vendor data, "the permit will renew": assumptions get recorded at sanction and rarely reviewed. An assumption is a constraint nobody has verified yet, and it fails the same way, late and expensively.

They go stale at different rates. Soil assumptions last years; a vendor's shop capacity changes weekly. Give each one an owner and a recheck date that fits how fast it can change.

Sep 11, 2026 scheduling metrics

Baselines Go Stale

The baseline records what we believed on sanction day. Two winters and forty change orders later, we still measure against it and call the difference variance.

Rebaselining feels like admitting a mistake, so it happens late, and until then reports measure distance from a plan nobody believes.

Keep every forecast. The baseline is the one tied to the contract. Track how good each forecast turned out to be, and rebaselining becomes routine.

The Whiteboard and the Schedule Drift Apart

The schedule has five thousand activities. The job runs off a whiteboard three weeks deep, updated at 6 a.m. Both are needed: the contract needs the full record, the crew needs this week.

The problem is that the whiteboard becomes the plan everyone follows and never updates the schedule. Within a season, the schedule describes a different job.

Treat them as two views of the same constraints, and let changes on the board flow back into the schedule.

Sep 10, 2026 constraints theory scheduling

Everything Is a Constraint

A predecessor, a missing spool, an unsigned permit, a crane booked elsewhere, wind over the lift limit, a room too crowded to work in, a dose budget almost used up. These are managed in different systems by different people, but they are the same thing: a condition that must be true before work can start.

CPM made logic ties the main type of constraint, mostly because that was what 1960s computers could handle. Ask a superintendent what stopped work this week and it is rarely a predecessor.

The schedule follows from the constraints

If everything is a constraint, the schedule is the result of the constraint set, not the plan itself. Dates are outputs. Managing dates instead of constraints means managing the result.

One type also means one description: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. A permit and a weather window differ only in how they're cleared. In one set, you can ask each morning which constraints to clear to free the most work.

I don't yet know whether one system can handle all of these well. But keeping them in separate systems costs a lot of coordination, for reasons that no longer apply.

Sep 10, 2026 constraints scheduling

Watch for Closing Windows

A pour needs three dry days. The crane leaves in October. In-water work stops when the fish return. None of these are logic ties.

Windows distort float. Forty days of float before a window that closes in forty-one days is really one day of float, and most tools won't show that. Miss the window and the delay becomes a season.

Treat each window as a constraint with an expiry date, and review the ones that are close every morning.

Sep 10, 2026 field scheduling

Some Resources Are Specific People

The schedule says two Class B welders. The job needs Dave, the only one qualified for that alloy, and Dave is at his daughter's wedding until Tuesday.

The histogram shows welders at sixty percent all month. That's true and doesn't help. Totals hide the single people that jobs depend on.

Name people where it matters: qualifications, signing authority, the operator for a tandem lift. Give them calendars, so the wedding shows up in the plan in March, when it's easy to work around.

Sep 10, 2026 field software

Capture Field Notes Once, in a Usable Shape

Foremen write down what happened every day: the night pour ran long, the pump was swapped at 3:10, form 12-B needs watching. Then the same facts get retyped into the schedule update, timesheet, RFI, QC record and claim file.

The fix is to capture it in a structured way to begin with: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. Recorded once like that, the other documents can be generated from it.

Claims teams already rebuild those four facts from diaries years later, at legal rates. That is the cost of unstructured notes.

Sep 10, 2026 cost theory

Use One Breakdown Structure

The estimate, the schedule, cost control and the work packages each have their own breakdown. They match at sanction. By month six there are four versions and a regular meeting to reconcile them.

Each group needs a different view: cost by contract, field by area, schedule by system. Those can all be views of one structure instead of separate copies.

Keep one breakdown and generate every rollup from it. Copies drift; references don't.

The reconciliation meeting only exists because of the copies.

Sep 10, 2026 trust field

Put a Name on Every Green Status

Anyone can mark a status green, and it costs nothing, so reports drift optimistic. Green often means nobody wanted the argument.

The field already solved this: a weld is accepted because a qualified person stamps it, and that stamp is theirs for good. Do the same with status. Ask for a name on every green. There will be fewer greens, and they'll mean more.

Sep 10, 2026 metrics

Measure How Certain the Forecast Is

SPI and CPI measure work done against the plan. Nothing measures how well we know the finish date, which is what the owner cares about. So reports can stay green while the job gets later.

Measure the uncertainty

At any status date, the finish is a range, say P10 to P90. A month that settled a question that had spread turnover across eight months may have done more for the schedule than a month of record quantities. Nothing currently measures that. Plan how fast the range should narrow, and track it.

Two proposed indices

SCI (schedule certainty index) = planned range width ÷ actual range width. 1.0 is on plan; above 1 is better than planned.

CCI (cost certainty index) = the same ratio for the estimate at completion.

The worrying case is quantities moving while the range doesn't narrow.

Stopping people from gaming it

Left alone, everyone will report a narrow range. So compare reported ranges against what happened, the way weather forecasts are scored, and penalize overconfidence more than admitted uncertainty. I don't know whether that would survive a steering committee.

Most large jobs already run a schedule risk analysis and then throw away the distribution. Keeping it would be a cheap experiment.
