Memes Are Meaningful

Hand-drawn cartoons on the trailer whiteboard. A Teams channel full of oddly specific GIFs. Inside jokes nobody outside the gate gets. That isn't a crew slacking off. That's a crew that gives a damn about each other.

The red flag is the dead-quiet channel and the spotless whiteboard. When nobody jokes, nobody shares context either. They clock in, cover their ass and keep their heads down.

Shared jokes · shared ownership

  • Memes are compressed site knowledge. One dumb GIF about the stubborn gate valve in Sector 4 carries more situational awareness than a three-page status memo. Everyone sees it, laughs, and remembers the trap.
  • Humour is the pressure valve. Brutal schedules, weather and shifting drawings grind people down. A crew that can roast the absurdity together without looking over its shoulder keeps talking.
  • HR can't mandate culture. A lunchroom poster builds nothing. Chasing a hard goal together, calling out each other's saves and inventing your own language does.

If the whiteboard is full of stick figures and the group chat is funny, leave it alone. Your team is locked in.

Embrace the Weirdos

Corporate culture sands everyone down into polite, predictable clones. On a job site, sanding off the weird edges throws away your best protection against disaster. The people who don't fit the mold usually carry exactly what you need when things go sideways.

Odd edges · sharper coverage

  • Lou's obsessive detail catches the bolt callout, the elevation mismatch, the permit condition everyone skimmed. Annoying when you're rushing. Priceless before you pour or energize.
  • Tina's contrarian questions poke holes in groupthink before a bad plan leaves the trailer. She's the one asking what happens when the crane goes down.
  • Paul's crustiness is scar tissue with a pulse. He's watched this "can't-miss" plan blow up three times since 1998. Listen past the grumbling and you get twenty years of pattern recognition free.
  • Maya's "lazy" shortcuts are ruthless efficiency. She spots the three extra handoffs and duplicate forms everyone else treats as sacred.

Don't turn Lou into a dreamer, Tina into a yes-man, Paul into HR-speak, or Maya into a lover of extra steps. Put each edge where it pays.

Most People Are Redeemable. A Tiny Handful Need to Get the Fuck Out.

Almost nobody on a bad job is broken. They're reacting to a broken system. Put the guy who looks lazy, cynical or checked out on a clean work front with real tools, open information and a boss who listens, and nine times out of ten he turns back into a builder. Fix the friction and most "problem employees" disappear.

Then there's the other two percent. They hoard information so others fail, lie about field conditions, play politics behind the foreman's back, cut safety corners, or treat people like dirt to feel big.

High trust · hard boundary

  • Good people watch who you tolerate. Every day a toxic operator slides, your best hands look a little harder at the gate.
  • Coaching doesn't fix malice. You can train skill and build confidence. You can't coach integrity into someone who enjoys breaking trust.
  • Fast exits protect the room. When someone crosses the line, cut them loose cleanly. The relief on site is immediate, and the standard means something.

Give ninety-eight percent room to grow and screw up. Show the two percent the gate without blinking.

Good Enough Is Often All You Need. Not Everything Is a Nuclear Valve.

Treating a laydown pad, a utility trench or an access road like a safety-critical reactor part burns time, money and attention you never get back. Perfectionism sounds noble until a team spends four days gold-plating a detail that doesn't move the milestone and won't matter once the next crew drives over it.

Right standard · zero gold-plating

  • Match rigour to consequence. Save zero-defect obsession for what bites: pressure boundaries, life safety, critical tie-ins, long-lead gear. Everywhere else, "holds, passes inspection, keeps the sequence moving" is a win.
  • Gold-plating steals oxygen. Every hour polishing a non-critical detail is an hour not spent clearing a real constraint.
  • Finished beats fussed-over. A solid job on time beats a masterpiece stuck shaving off the last two percent.

Know when you're building a nuclear valve, and when you're building a good-enough bridge across the creek.

Clear Expectations Include Tolerances

Vague instructions aren't the problem. Fake precision is. "I need this ASAP" or "stage the gear over there", when you mean "sometime next week, earlier's a bonus, anywhere that doesn't block the trailer", forces everyone to play mind-reader.

Engineers put tolerances on every drawing: ±5 mm, a class of fit, an allowable deflection. Then leaders hand out tasks with no error bars at all, in the same tone whether it's a life-safety hold point or a rough staging request. A tolerance is just a confidence range handed to the person doing the work, instead of a fake point estimate.

Stated error bars · zero mind-reading

  • Time: "Sometime next week works. Earlier is great; no sweat if it slides a couple of days." ASAP turns every minor task into a fake emergency that burns the real ones.
  • Place and method: "Stage it near the break trailer. Use your judgment so we don't block access."
  • Consequence: "Hydrovac for a day to buy confidence on the copper phone lines. Nobody dies if we nick one; maintenance just gets pissed and we eat a service headache." That's the sweet spot between digging blind and a week of over-caution.

When certainty is low, the range stays wide. As drawings firm up and locates come back clean, it narrows on its own. Until then, give people the real shape of the target, then get out of the way.

Activities Have Ranges, Not Hard-Coded Durations

Typing "3 to 5 days" into a Gantt bar isn't the fix. The activity itself has a range: how much ground it covers, what it includes, how far it pushes. Hard-coding a duration onto fuzzy work is the lie.

Define the bounds · let the duration fall out

  • Effort: "Hydrovac up to a day. Stop when you can see the layout; don't chase every inch."
  • Place: "Somewhere around the break trailer, clear of the door and the traffic lane."
  • Window: "Sometime next week, when the front is open and it doesn't collide with harder work."

The duration becomes whatever it takes to work that range in real conditions. The hard part is setting the ceiling, so a crew doesn't spin past the point of diminishing returns.

Float Is Never Accurate. So Spend More Time Making It Accurate.

Scheduling software treats float like a bank balance: a clean number telling you how much slack you have. On a real job it's fiction the moment a logic tie is a bit off, a crew gets pulled sideways, a permit lags, or an activity has a range instead of a fixed bar.

Bad float · false confidence

  • Fake float hides traps. Eight days of cushion, so everyone ignores it, until a hidden dependency eats all eight before anyone looks up.
  • Fake zero float triggers panic. Sloppy logic or padded durations make a path look critical, and people chase ghosts instead of protecting the real choke points.
  • Fixing float is checking reality. Question the ties. Sort real constraints from invented ones. Find where slack actually exists in the work, not on the screen.

Wrong float is worse than no float. Spend the time to make it true.

Milestones Aren't All Equal

The software draws every milestone as the same diamond. A contractual submittal date gets the same symbol as energizing switchgear or pulling a crane off site. Treat every diamond like a five-alarm fire and crews learn that none of them matter.

Real gates · paper checkmarks

  • Physical gates vs reporting theatre. Some milestones open work fronts, release major equipment or cross irreversible safety lines. Others feed a progress slide or a billing box.
  • Hard constraints vs soft targets. Missing the window before freeze-up or a utility outage costs months. Slipping an internal rough-in date by three days when the next trade isn't mobilized costs nothing, unless someone panics into sloppy overtime.
  • Protect the hinges, flex the rest. A good field leader knows the three milestones that hold the job together and gives honest room around everything else.

Name the real hinges. Let the noise stay noise.

Your Suppliers Are Part of Your Team, or You're an Asshole and Will Fail

Hide the real schedule from your suppliers, scream when a delivery slips, keep them in the dark until you need a miracle, and you go straight to the bottom of their priority list. They're holding half your critical path in their shop, their yard or on the back of a flatbed.

Shared context · real priority

  • Give them the real picture. Suppliers know when a "drop-dead" date is padding. Tell them the true install window and where you have room, and they'll tell you the truth when their shop jams up.
  • Pull them in before it's a claim. A garbage detail or a shifted field dimension, raised early with the fabricator, saves weeks of RFIs and wrong parts.
  • Respect their time like your crew's. No trucks idling four hours at the gate because nobody cleared laydown. No Friday-afternoon emergencies you saw coming three weeks ago.

Shoot straight and they'll move mountains when the real emergency hits. Act like an asshole and you get exact contract compliance and zero favours.

Home-Grown Talent Beats High-Priced Consulting (Usually)

Paying $350 an hour for an outsider in a crisp hard hat to tell you what your superintendents already know is one of the most expensive ways to waste time on a job. By the time they learn how your permits work, which substation feeds which line and why the north laydown floods every spring, your people could have solved it twice.

Earned context · skin in the game

  • Context beats credentials. Your lead already knows the unwritten rules and who actually decides. The outsider bills you to learn the basics.
  • Your people live with the outcome. They walk past that valve next month. Consultants leave a binder and fly out before the concrete cures.
  • Bypassing your own people kills morale. Nothing says "your judgment doesn't count" like paying triple to someone who interviews them and repeats their ideas to management.

The "usually" matters. For a one-off niche skill, a forensic metallurgist or a one-time regulatory witness, bring in the specialist, get the answer, cut them loose. For running the work, bet on the people who know your dirt.

Everyone Needs a Desk and a Locker

Gear in a car trunk, a laptop on your knees in a noisy hallway, paperwork on a gangbox: the message is you're temporary, and your time doesn't matter. A desk and a locker, even shared ones, aren't perks. They're tools.

Basic dignity · real ownership

  • Shared beats nothing. A clean shared desk with a monitor, a decent chair and real Wi-Fi beats perching on a crate. A locker that locks beats tools rolling around a pickup bed.
  • Include the people who hold the site together. The cleaner, the yard hand, the material coordinator, the apprentice. Sticking them in a broom closet with broken gear isn't saving money; it's being a cheap prick.
  • Space signals belonging. Somewhere to put boots, hard hat, notebook and lunch without worrying about theft, and people act like they belong on the job.

No marble floors needed. Just enough honest desks and lockers that nobody feels like an afterthought.

Timesheet Extortion

Data collection schemes die the same way: the form is optional, the foreman is busy, the form loses. One form never loses. People make sure they get paid.

So attach status to the timesheet. To submit hours against a task, answer for the task. Call it extortion. It has a hundred-percent completion rate.

One tap per task · then it pays

The toll has to be a few minutes, so no essays. Done, partial, or blocked — one tap. Partial: most, half, a little. Blocked: the task's own open constraints appear as a list — tap the one, or "new" with a photo. The choices come from the model, so every tap goes back into it.

A few minutes at quitting time, priced into the day — and the schedule updates every night because everyone wanted to get paid. Runs on the company phone.

The Two-Hour Wall

One sentence in the fire code: this wall stops fire for two hours. Follow it. It becomes a line in the design basis, a wall type on a drawing, a firestop detail, a can of foam and a QC stamp, a commissioning walkdown. Then the project leaves, and for the next forty years electricians drill holes in it.

Every phase retypes the sentence into its own documents and keeps them. By year ten the guy cutting a cable tray through that wall has no idea it's rated. The requirement is still law. Nobody standing at the wall has ever seen it.

One constraint · sixty years

Keep it as one constraint: legislated, designed, built, inspected, commissioned, maintained. Everybody points at it. Nobody retypes it. Then the electrician can ask the wall why he can't drill here and get the real answer, and the regulator can ask which walls this clause owns and when they were last checked. Two good questions, one up and one down. Today both are a records search.

The 200-Day Bar

Scaffold inspections, permit renewals, dewatering checks — every Tuesday for a year. The scheduler draws one 200-day bar.

The bar has real jobs: it loads the histogram, holds the work area, collects level-of-effort cost. What it can't do is fail. Never late, never critical, never missed — and this Tuesday's inspection can be.

The bar never fails · the Tuesday does

Late looks like this: the tag expires Thursday at 16:00, Friday three crews have no legal platform, and their timesheets say low productivity. The miss is real — booked to the victims. Derive the instances instead. Each is a constraint with an expiry, so Wednesday you already know which tag lapses tomorrow and who loses their platform. The bar notices nothing.

STKY³

STKY — stuff that kills you — is safety's hard-won discipline: attention goes to what seriously injures and kills, not to papercuts. That stays sacred. Borrow it for a second ledger: what kills the project.

The risk matrix has two axes, probability and severity. The third is reversibility. A loss that bleeds over three years has a dozen intervention points; the same loss can turn permanent in one afternoon, with one signature. Where severe meets irreversible, probability stops mattering.

In the corner, probability stops mattering

Plotted that way it stops being a worry list and becomes a controls map: it says where a person has to be standing. Nuclear operations already does this before touching a valve — critical-step identification. Take it upstream, into construction and project decisions. Papercuts still aggregate — that's a different ledger — they just don't get to spend fatal-risk attention.

Papercuts Are Data

STKY comes first — attention goes to what kills. But nobody wants the nicks and strains either.

Recording one takes far longer than treating it, so it never gets recorded. Ten seconds, or the data doesn't happen.

Record in ten seconds, or not at all

Then trend: nicks against the blade policy, strains by area, week over week. The worst class gets an owner and a control change. Papercuts don't predict deaths. They will happen twice for the same reason.

Crews Have Memory

The plan prices labor as a rate. A crew that's poured together all season beats one assembled Monday — same trade, same rate, different job.

The difference lives in the crew: the operator who knows the rigger's hands, the finisher nobody has to sequence. Schedule capital, recorded nowhere — so the leveling run treats six welders as six interchangeable pegs and spends it for free.

Crews have memory

Splitting a good crew is a schedule decision that appears in no schedule. Names matter — and some come in sets.

Weather Is a Bitch

The only project risk with a free, public, daily probabilistic forecast — and plans still treat a January pour like July with a note attached.

Two free feeds, both ignored. Thirty years of records say how many pour days February actually holds: plan on February's distribution, not February's average. And the ten-day forecast is a live constraint feed — when Thursday crosses the wind limit, the lift's window just moved, and the model should re-derive like it would for any constraint expiring.

Free forecast · unused

Instead the forecast lives on the super's phone, and the schedule finds out in the delay notice.

Sep 11, 2026 · TW metrics theory

Unknown Unknowns

You can't list them. You can count them arriving: new constraints surfaced this week — the discovery rate.

It should fall as the job learns. The trap: a job that stops surprising you is done learning, or done looking — and the falling line looks the same either way. So count the looking too: walkdowns, assumption rechecks, questions asked. Discoveries per look is the honest number.

Discoveries per look

Discoveries falling while the looking holds steady: the fog is burning off, and the window should be narrowing to match. Both falling together: nobody's learning anything — the surprises are saving themselves up.

The Morning Question

Status meetings ask what happened. The job moves on which constraints, killed today, release the most work?

That's a ranking, and nothing on site ranks it. Lists sort by date raised or by owner — never by hours released.

A good superintendent runs the ranking in their head at 05:30 and is usually right. Build that, shared. Nothing smarter than the super — just always awake.

The Critical Path Is a Coin Flip

Run the same schedule through a thousand weathers and deliveries and a different path goes critical in a third of the runs. The red line is one draw.

And it moves every update, so crews get whipsawed toward whatever went red this month. Critical in sixty of a hundred futures deserves attention every month — whichever path the printout crowned.

Critical, most mornings

Float answers a question about one run. Criticality answers a question about the job.

Assumed True Until Checked

Ground conditions, vendor data, "the permit will renew" — logged at sanction, reread never. An assumption is a constraint someone chose not to verify yet. It fails like one: without warning, late, where change is dearest.

Unwatched · not untrue yet

They age at different speeds — soil over years, a vendor's shop loading week to week. Give each an owner and a recheck date scaled to its half-life. A register that can ring.

The baseline is what we believed on sanction day. Two winters and forty change orders later, we still measure against it and call the gap variance.

Everything Is a Constraint

A predecessor tie. A missing spool. An unsigned permit. A crane booked elsewhere. Forecast wind over the lift limit. A room too crowded to work in. A dose budget nearly spent. We manage these in seven different systems with seven different owners, and they are the same object: a condition that must be true before work can proceed.

CPM made one constraint type — logic ties — the aristocracy, and demoted the rest to footnotes. Not because logic matters most, but because it was the type a 1960s computer could hold. Sixty years later the field still runs on that accident. Ask any superintendent what actually stopped work this week; it is almost never a predecessor.

Seven costumes · one object

The schedule is a shadow

If everything is a constraint, then the schedule isn't the plan — it's the shadow the constraint set casts on a calendar. Dates are outputs. Managing the dates instead of the constraints is managing the shadow and wondering why it won't hold still.

One object also means one grammar: what it blocks, what satisfies it, who owns it, when it expires. A permit and a weather window differ only in their satisfaction function. Put them in one set and you can finally ask the only question that matters each morning — which constraints, if I killed them today, release the most work?

Whether one engine can hold all seven types honestly, I'm still finding out. But I've stopped believing the seven-systems version is anything other than a coordination tax we pay for a hardware limit that expired before I was born.

A window is a constraint with an expiry, not a note in a drawer. The cliff-edged ones go on the board every morning.

Write the Story Once

Every foreman writes the story every day: night pour ran long, pump swap at 03:10, watch form 12-B. Then five systems demand the same facts retyped — schedule update, timesheet, RFI, QC record, claim file.

The fix is structure at capture, not software reading diaries. ACE — atomic constraint entry: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. Told once in that shape, the update, the RFI and the claim file become queries.

Written once · projected five times

Claims teams already rebuild those four fields from prose, years later, at lawyer rates. That's the invoice for capturing stories without structure.

Sep 10, 2026 · TW metrics

Two Numbers Your Project Doesn't Track

SPI and CPI score effort against the plan. Nothing scores the forecast. The owner is buying a date; nobody measures whether we know it. So reports stay green while the job gets later.

Measure the fog

At any status date the finish is a P10–P90 window. A month that poured nothing but closed the question smearing turnover across eight months bought more schedule than a record month of quantities — and no metric noticed. So baseline how fast the window should shrink, and track the shrink. That might be the real progress. Might.

SCI and CCI

SCI = planned window width ÷ actual. 1.0 on plan, above is ahead.

CCI = the same ratio on the estimate-at-completion spread.

SPI across · SCI up

The circled corner is the one that worries me: quantities moving, fog not lifting. Today that manager probably gets promoted.

The anti-gaming rule

Left alone, everyone claims a narrow window. So claimed windows get scored against realized dates, the way weather forecasters are graded — overconfidence should cost more than admitted doubt. Which means a window that widens honestly can be the best number on the report. Whether that survives contact with a steering committee, I don't know.

A year of window width is a year of financed capital, and contingency is the posted price of not knowing — so this is probably worth real money. I won't defend a number. The engines already exist: every big job runs a schedule risk analysis and throws the distribution away. Keeping it is a cheap experiment.
