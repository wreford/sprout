Done Should Be Calculated, Not Typed

On most status reports, done is a number somebody types. It drifts up, because nobody wants the argument, and then sits at 95 percent for months (the last 5 percent).

Define done by the evidence that proves it. A wall is done when its inspection is signed, its deficiencies are closed and its test record is filed. A system is done when every part of it is. Nobody types it. It follows from the records.

What changes

  • Arguments move to records. "It's done" becomes "which signature is missing?" (signatures).
  • Partial progress stops being a guess. Count the evidence that exists against the evidence required.
  • Closeout shows up early, as missing evidence on items everyone thought were finished.
  • Rollups can't drift. A floor is done when its rooms are, and a building when its floors are (one breakdown).

Try it on one area. List what has to be true for each item to count as done, and mark only what you can point to. The first report will show less progress than last week's. That's the accurate one.

Every Open Item Should Explain Itself

Ask why a task isn't finished and the answer usually takes three phone calls. The crew is waiting on scaffold. Scaffold is waiting on a permit. The permit is waiting on a signature from someone on holiday.

Somebody knows each of those links. None of them is written down where the next person can follow it.

Follow the chain to a name

For every open item, record what it is waiting on, then what that is waiting on, until you reach a person and a date. "Why isn't this done?" then has an answer anyone can read in a minute, without asking around.

Two things fall out. Every chain ends at a named person, so nothing waits on "them". And chains that end at the same blocker show where clearing one thing frees the most work. The chains also show where handoffs are slowing things down, and how long the news took to arrive.

It works because everything blocking work is the same kind of thing: a condition that has to be true first.

Try it in the next coordination meeting. For each red item, keep asking what it's waiting on until you reach a name, and write the chain down. Most meetings never get past the first link.

Price Every Risk in Days

A heat map puts a risk in a red square. It doesn't say what the risk would do to the finish date, so the meeting argues about colours.

Price it instead. Run the schedule many times with realistic variation in durations and deliveries, once with the risk and once without. The difference in the finish date is what that risk costs, in days, and in dollars at your daily cost of delay.

  • The list sorts itself. A likely risk on a path with float may cost nothing. An unlikely one on a path that is often critical may cost weeks.
  • Timing counts. The same delay costs more just before a closing window.
  • Responses get a price too. If a mitigation costs less than the days it saves, do it.
  • Assumptions are risks. Price the ones you haven't checked.

Severe, irreversible losses still need judgment on top of the number (reversibility).

Most large jobs already run this kind of analysis once, for the owner, then file it. Run it monthly and use the result to rank the risk register.

A Crane Is a Predecessor Too

Two lifts that need the same crane can't happen at once. That tie is as firm as rebar before concrete. Most schedules keep logic in one place and resources in another, and reconcile them at the end, if at all.

So the schedule shows both lifts on Tuesday, and the crane operator decides which one waits.

One kind of link

Treat shared capacity as a link between activities: these two need the same crane, the same welder, the same room, the same outage. Then the sequence you see is the one the site will run. It's the same idea as everything being a constraint: capacity is one more condition that has to be free first.

It explains float that disappears on site: the logic said eight days, but the crane didn't. It also explains why the critical path often runs through one busy resource instead of through the logic. And crews are shared capacity too.

Try it on the next three-week lookahead. List every crane, hoist, specialist and shutdown window, and draw a line between activities that share one. The conflicts are hard to miss once they're drawn.

Write the Site Rules Once

No hot work within 10 metres of fuel. Two people for confined space entry. Hold for the inspector before backfill. These rules are in a binder, a toolbox talk and the superintendent's head. The plan doesn't know any of them.

So a lookahead can put welding next to a fuel delivery, and someone has to notice.

Rules the plan can check

Write each rule as a check against the plan: which activities it applies to, and what has to be true before they go ahead. Run the checks every time the plan changes, and a conflict shows up on the lookahead days before the crew arrives.

Rules written once stop depending on who's on shift, and they don't get lost at a handoff. Change a rule in one place and every future plan follows the new version, the same way procedures written as steps do. Requirements like the two-hour wall can be checked the same way for the life of the building.

Start with the rules that guard against the worst outcomes, and the hold points that need a signature to release. Each rule is a constraint like any other.

Try it with the five rules behind your last few near misses. Check next week's lookahead against them by hand and count the conflicts.

The Last 5 Percent

Punch lists, commissioning, turnover documents and closeout often take a third of the calendar. They get planned as if they were the last 5 percent of the work, and they start getting managed when the crews are leaving.

By then the people who knew the systems have moved on, the records are scattered across the files, and every remaining item depends on somebody else.

Start it on day one

  • Give every system a turnover package and an owner from the start. Fill it as the work is done: test records, certificates, signed inspections, field notes.
  • Close deficiencies as they appear, each with a photo or short video, instead of collecting 1,400 at the final walkthrough.
  • Schedule commissioning as work, with durations and logic, not as a single diamond at the end.
  • Don't report 95 percent as nearly done. A green status at 95 percent hides the slowest third of the job, and the bad news arrives at the end.

Use the as-built schedules from past jobs to see how long closeout took. It is usually far longer than the plan said.

The last 5 percent is where the owner forms a final opinion of the job, and where the retention money is held.

Keep the As-Built Schedule

At closeout, the start and finish of every activity gets archived with the project files and is never opened again. It is the best estimating data a contractor owns: real production rates, on its own jobs, with its own crews, in its own climate.

The next estimate starts from published rates and someone's memory instead. That's why the same activities come in light, job after job.

What to keep

  • Durations and quantities. Planned against as-built for every activity, with the quantity installed, so you get a rate: units per crew-day.
  • Why the big gaps happened. The weather days, the blocked shifts, the rework. A slow activity with a known cause tells you something different from one with no explanation.
  • Who did the work. Crews that stayed together ran faster, and some activities depended on one person.

You can only compare jobs if they share a breakdown structure, and the data is only cheap to keep if it was captured once while the work happened.

Kept that way, it becomes your own reference class. Estimates start from what happened here before, ranges come from real spreads instead of guesses, and float sits on durations you can defend. Forecasts can be scored against it. It also shows how long the last 5 percent takes on your jobs.

The Cost of Asking

Every site has a price for asking a question. Part of it is time: finding the person who knows, then waiting for them to answer. Part of it is social: looking like you should already know. When the price is high, people guess, and some of those guesses get built.

What makes asking expensive

  • Nobody obvious to ask. The requirement is buried in a document the electrician will never see (the two-hour wall). The person who can decide is away and nobody else can sign (delegation, signatures).
  • Asking looks bad. Where people expect blame, they stay quiet (problem employees). Quiet sites ask less (inside jokes). The person who asks awkward questions in meetings gets treated as a nuisance (odd people).
  • The answer comes too late. An RFI that takes three weeks teaches crews to stop sending them. Every handoff adds a wait (handoffs).
  • Outsiders don't know who to ask. A consultant spends a month finding out what the site team knows (consultants).

Make it cheap

Track two numbers: how many questions get asked, and how long they take to answer. More questions is a good sign. Slow answers are the problem.

Give instructions with their tolerances so fewer questions are needed. Answer the ones that block the most work first (the morning question). Treat an unanswered question like any other constraint: it holds up work until it's cleared.

Bad news has to travel up. Questions have to travel down and across. Both slow down at every handoff.

Count the Handoffs

Schedules count tasks. Most of what goes wrong happens between tasks, where work, information or authority passes from one person, crew, company or system to the next.

Each handoff can drop something:

  • The requirement. The fire rating is in the design basis, the detail and the QC record, and missing from what the electrician sees ten years later (the two-hour wall).
  • The item. A spool that changes hands six times can go missing at any of them (material).
  • The authority. A signer on holiday stops the next crew (delegation, signatures).
  • The intent. "ASAP" arrives without the tolerance behind it (tolerances).
  • The context. A crew gets split up, or a consultant leaves with what they learned (crews, consultants).
  • Time. Every handoff adds days before bad news reaches the plan.

Fewer, then explicit

Count handoffs per work package the way you count activities. Remove the ones that exist only because the same data is kept in separate copies (one breakdown, capture once). Make the rest explicit: a scan at the handoff, a signature with a name, steps that travel with the work. Suppliers are a handoff too, and the one you control least.

The whiteboard and the schedule are a handoff that happens every morning. Named people make the handoffs that depend on one person visible before they fail.

A schedule with fewer tasks isn't simpler if it has more handoffs.

How Long Bad News Takes to Reach the Plan

Every problem has two dates: when someone on site first saw it, and when it showed up in the plan. Call the gap bad-news latency. On most jobs it runs to days or weeks, and for all of that time decisions are being made from a plan that is wrong.

Nobody tracks it, so nobody manages it. Measuring it is simple: log when each new constraint was first seen and when it entered the plan, and watch the median gap week to week.

Where the days go

  • Seeing it and saying it. Quiet crews don't pass things on (inside jokes), people who expect blame keep quiet (problem employees), and small injuries go unreported (minor injuries).
  • Writing it down. If capture takes more than a minute, it waits for Friday. A tap on the timesheet, a phone and a thirty-second video record it on the spot, once.
  • Getting it into the plan. The whiteboard knows by 6 a.m. The schedule finds out at the monthly update, too late for the morning question.
  • Partners. A supplier who has been given padded dates sits on their own delays (suppliers).
  • Reporting. Unsigned green status and stale baselines hide what someone already knows.

Why the delay is expensive

A week of delay on most news costs about a week. On a closing window it can cost a season. On a decision that can't be reversed, the news may arrive after the decision is made. And the forecast range can't narrow faster than new problems reach the plan.

Most fixes in these notes shorten one stage of the delay. Measuring the whole delay shows which stage is slowest on your job.

Inside Jokes Are a Good Sign

A crew that draws cartoons on the trailer whiteboard is a crew that talks. The GIF about the sticking valve in Sector 4 does more to keep people away from that valve than the line in last month's report.

Worry about the quiet site. If nobody jokes, nobody is passing on what they know either.

If the whiteboard is full of stick figures, leave it up.

Put Odd People Where They Fit

The person who annoys everyone by checking every bolt callout is the one who catches the wrong grade before the pour. The one who argues in every meeting is the one asking what happens when the crane goes down.

Most difficult people have one habit that is very valuable in the right job and tiresome in the wrong one. Don't train it out of them. Move them to where it pays.

Most Problem Employees Are Reacting to the Job

Give someone who looks checked out a ready work front, the right tools and a straight answer when they ask, and most of them start working well. A lot of bad attitudes are reasonable responses to a badly run job.

A few people do damage on purpose: they hide information, misreport progress, or take safety shortcuts with other people's lives. Remove them quickly. Everyone else is watching what you tolerate.

Match the Rigour to the Consequence

A temporary laydown pad does not need the paperwork of a pressure boundary. Spending four days perfecting it costs four days on work that drives the finish.

Decide early which work carries real consequence: life safety, pressure retention, critical tie-ins, long-lead equipment. Hold that to the highest standard. For everything else, the standard is that it works, passes inspection and doesn't hold up the next crew. Say which is which out loud, so crews don't have to guess.

Give Instructions With Tolerances

Drawings say ±5 mm. Instructions say "ASAP". The crew can't tell a hold point from a favour, so they treat everything as urgent and get worn out by work that wasn't.

Give the range you'd accept:

  • When: "Next week. A couple of days late is fine."
  • Where: "Somewhere near the break trailer that doesn't block the door."
  • What happens if it goes wrong: "If you nick the phone line it's a repair call, not an injury."

People make good calls when they know how much room they have.

Define the Activity, Not Just Its Duration

"Hydrovac the copper lines, 3 days" invites three days of digging whether you need it or not. "Hydrovac until you can see the layout, a day at most" tells the crew when they're done.

For uncertain work, set the stopping point and the limit. The duration follows. Without a stopping point, work expands to fill the bar.

Check Your Float

Float is only as good as the logic under it. One missing tie and an activity shows eight days of slack it doesn't have. One padded duration and a path looks critical when it isn't.

Both errors cost you: the first gets ignored until it bites, the second gets resources it doesn't need. Before trusting a float number, walk the logic behind it with the people doing the work.

Not All Milestones Matter Equally

A submittal date and first energization get the same diamond on the schedule. They are not the same. Miss the freeze-up window and you lose a season. Miss an internal rough-in target by three days with the next trade not mobilized, and nothing happens.

Name the three or four milestones the job depends on. Protect those. If every date is treated as critical, crews learn that none are.

Treat Suppliers Like Part of the Team

Suppliers know when your "drop-dead" date has two weeks of padding in it. Lie to them and they stop telling you about the problems in their shop until it's too late to work around them.

Give them the actual install date and where you have slack. Call them in when a detail looks wrong, before it becomes a string of RFIs. Clear laydown before the truck arrives.

When something goes wrong, the supplier who trusts you finds a way. The one you've mistreated sends you the contract.

Use Your Own People Before Consultants

A consultant at $350 an hour spends the first month learning what your superintendent already knows: which permits are slow, which substation feeds what, why the north yard floods in spring. Then the report repeats what the site team said in the first interview.

Your people live with the result. Consultants are worth it for narrow, rare problems: a forensic metallurgist, a one-off regulatory witness. For running the work, invest in the people already there.

Everything Is a Constraint

A predecessor, a missing spool, an unsigned permit, a crane booked elsewhere, wind over the lift limit, a room too crowded to work in, a dose budget nearly spent. Seven systems manage them. They're all the same thing: a condition that must be true before work can start.

CPM made logic ties the main constraint because that was what 1960s computers could handle. Ask a superintendent what stopped work this week and it is rarely a predecessor.

Dates are outputs

If everything is a constraint, the schedule is what the constraints produce, not the plan itself. Each one has the same fields: what it blocks, what clears it, who owns it, when it expires. A permit and a weather window differ only in how they get cleared.

Keep them in one place and you can ask each morning which ones to clear to free the most work. I don't know yet whether one system can hold all seven types well. The cost of keeping them in seven is clear.

Measure How Certain the Forecast Is

SPI and CPI measure work done against the plan. Nothing measures how well we know the finish date, which is what the owner is paying for. A job can report green every month while it gets later.

Track the range

At any status date the finish is a range, say P10 to P90. Settling a question that had spread turnover across eight months can be worth more than a record month of quantities. Plan how fast the range should narrow, then track it.

SCI (schedule certainty index) = planned range width ÷ actual range width. Above 1 is better than planned.

CCI (cost certainty index) = the same for the estimate at completion.

The worrying case is quantities moving while the range stays wide.

Scoring the ranges

Left alone, everyone reports a narrow range. So score reported ranges against what happened, the way weather forecasters are scored, and penalize overconfidence more than admitted doubt. Most big jobs already run a schedule risk analysis and then discard the distribution. Keeping it is a cheap experiment.
