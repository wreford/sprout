Two Numbers Your Project Doesn't Track

metrics evm certainty

A schedule is not a plan of work. It is a forecast wearing a plan's clothing. The owner is not buying a Gantt chart; they are buying a prediction of when the asset exists. Yet every standard metric we run — SPI, CPI, percent complete — measures the effort against the plan, and none of them measures the quality of the prediction itself.

This produces a failure mode every megaproject manager has lived through: months where the reports are green and the project is somehow getting later. Quantities move on paths with years of float while the one vendor issue that actually decides the end date sits untouched. SPI applauds. The finish date drifts right. Earned value is structurally blind to this because it was built to measure effort, and on a major project effort is not the scarce resource. Certainty is.

Measure the fog, not just the work

At any status date, the completion date is not a point; it is a distribution. There is a window of plausible finishes — call it the P10–P90 spread. That window has a width, the width changes every month, and its change is the most important schedule fact on the project. A month that installed nothing but killed the ambiguity smearing fuel load across an eight-month window made real schedule progress. A month of heroic quantities that left the fog exactly where it was made none.

So baseline it. Alongside the milestone dates, commit at sanction to a planned certainty profile: by month 18 the completion window will be down to six months; by month 36, eight weeks. Nobody baselines this today, and it is arguably the more meaningful promise — a date is wrong the day it is printed, but a certainty profile is a claim about how fast the project will learn, and it can be scored.

SCI and CCI

Schedule Certainty Index: planned window width divided by actual window width at the status date. On plan is 1.0, ahead is above, exactly like the indices everyone already knows. Cost Certainty Index: the same ratio on the estimate-at-completion distribution. The whole earned-value vocabulary ports over — certainty baseline, certainty earned, certainty variance — with confidence as the currency instead of budgeted cost of work.

Read them as a quadrant against SPI and CPI. High SPI, high SCI: genuinely on track. High SPI, low SCI: the dangerous quadrant — quantities moving, fog not lifting, green and getting later. Low SPI, high SCI: a project eating its risk early, often the healthiest state on the books and currently punished by every metric in the standard. Low both: actual trouble. The third quadrant alone justifies the extension, because today we fire that project manager and promote the one in quadrant two.

The anti-gaming rule

Any metric this powerful will be gamed, and the obvious move is to claim a narrow window with a straight face. So narrowness alone can never score. Claimed distributions are scored against realized dates with a proper scoring rule — the same mathematics weather forecasters live under — so that overconfidence costs more than admitted uncertainty. A tight window that proves wrong torches the index retroactively. Sandbagging and hero-dating both become losing strategies, which is a sentence no current schedule metric can say.

What certainty is worth

On a nuclear-scale build, certainty is not a soft benefit. Each year inside the completion window carries capital at billions per year and defers gigawatt-scale revenue behind it. Lenders do not price the expected date; they price the width of the distribution, and cost of capital is the dominant term in nuclear economics. Contingency — ten to fifteen percent of everything — is the explicit line-item price of not knowing. Stack the channels and certainty is plausibly worth a fifth of total project value. It is the most valuable unmanaged asset on the job, and no standard metric so much as glances at it.

The engines to compute all this already exist. The industry runs schedule risk analyses on every major project, presents the P80 once, and throws the distribution away. The proposal here is mostly to stop discarding the output of a machine we already own — and to start promising, and scoring, how fast the fog comes down.
