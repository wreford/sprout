Lessons Learned That Change the Next Job

Most lessons-learned work ends up as a report nobody reads. A few habits turn lessons into changes the next job will use.

Why the usual way fails

The usual way goes like this. At closeout, the project manager books a half-day session. Whoever is still on the job attends. The facilitator asks what went well and what didn't. The answers get typed into a report: communicate better with design, start procurement earlier, involve operations sooner. The report goes into the closeout files and, sometimes, a company database.

Then the next job starts, with a different team, and does the same things in the same way. Not because the team doesn't care, but because the lessons were never put anywhere the next team would run into them.

There are four common problems:

  • Too late. By closeout, the people who lived through the early phases have gone.
  • Too vague. "Communicate better" can't be done on a Monday morning.
  • Too far from the work. A database is somewhere you have to go and look. Nobody looks.
  • No owner. The report has findings but no one whose job it is to change anything.

Capture early and small

Hold a short session at the end of each phase: foundations, structure, envelope, systems, commissioning, handover. Thirty minutes. Invite the people who did the work, including the foremen. See Hold Lessons Sessions at the End of Each Phase.

Ask three questions:

  1. What surprised us?
  2. If we started this phase again on Monday, what would we do differently that day?
  3. Which document, checklist or template should change so the next job does that by default?

The second question forces concrete answers. The third makes them stick. See Ask What You'd Do Differently on Day One.

Turn lessons into defaults

A lesson is learned when the next job does the right thing without having to remember it. That means it lives in something the next team uses anyway:

  • the estimate checklist ("allow for temporary power to month 18");
  • the schedule template ("utility application in month 1, with its own steps");
  • a procedure hold point ("spray-test the first window of each type before finishes");
  • a site rule the plan is checked against ("no deck work past a scaffold tag expiry");
  • the startup meeting agenda ("which three milestones does this job live or die on?").

Give each change an owner and a date, the same as any other open item. Review them at the next phase session.

Learn from near misses and small things

The biggest lessons don't always come from the biggest failures. A near miss carries the same lesson as an injury, for free. A detail that leaked once in a mockup would have leaked four hundred times on the building. Count the small things and look for repeats. See Review Serious Near Misses Like Injuries and Build a Mockup of the Hard Details.

Use your own history

The as-built schedule and the cost records from past jobs are lessons in numbers: how long closeout took, how fast the crews poured, how late the utility was. Keep them in a shape you can compare across jobs, and start the next estimate from them. See Use Old As-Built Schedules for Estimating.

A simple measure

Count how many template, checklist and procedure changes came out of each job, and how many of them the next job used. If the number is zero, the process is producing reports, not learning. It's easy to track.
