Why Closeout Takes a Third of the Job

Deficiency lists, commissioning and turnover get planned as the last 5 percent of the work and managed when the crews are leaving. Here is why that goes wrong and how to run it from the start.

Feb 8, 2026 · 2 min read

The pattern

The structure tops out. The envelope closes. The progress report says 90 percent, then 95. Then it sits at 95 for months.

What is happening in those months is closeout: finishing the deficiency list, testing and commissioning the systems, gathering the documents the owner needs, training the operators, and handing over. On many jobs it takes a third of the calendar, sometimes more. The schedule allowed a few weeks. See The Last 5 Percent Takes a Third of the Job.

Why it takes so long

  • It was planned as a milestone, not work. Commissioning shows up as a diamond, so nobody resourced it.
  • The people have gone. The foremen who installed the systems are on other jobs. The people left have to learn the building to finish it.
  • The records are scattered. Test reports, certificates and inspection sign-offs are in a dozen places. Assembling them takes months. See The Two-Hour Wall.
  • The deficiency list was collected, not cleared. Thousands of small deficiencies get found on one final walk, each needing a different trade to come back.
  • Systems depend on each other. You can't balance the air until the controls work, and you can't finish the controls until the power is permanent. See Crossrail for this at national scale.
  • Percent complete hid it. Typed progress drifted up and stopped meaning anything. See Stop Typing Percent Complete.
Closeout is where the owner forms a final opinion of you, and where the holdback is held.

Run it from day one

Organize by system from the start. Give every system a turnover package and an owner in the first month. Every test record, certificate, signed inspection and photo goes into it as the work happens, not at the end.

Define done by evidence. A system is done when its package is complete, not when someone says so. Count the evidence on file against the evidence required, and progress stops drifting.

Clear deficiencies as they appear. Each with a photo, an owner and a date, closed while the trade is still on site.

Schedule commissioning as real work. With durations, logic, a team and the dependencies between systems drawn out. Start it as early as each system allows.

Keep some of the people. Hold back key foremen and engineers for closeout, and budget for it.

Staff the first month after handover. The owner remembers week one. See Owners Remember the First Week After Handover.

Measure it

Look at how long closeout took on your last three jobs, from the as-built schedules. That is your planning number, whatever the template says. Then track three things monthly from the start: turnover evidence on file against required, open deficiencies and their age, and systems through each commissioning stage. If those move steadily from early in the job, the last 5 percent stays the last 5 percent.
