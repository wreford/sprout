A Short History of Scheduling, From Gantt to Monte Carlo

Every tool on a scheduler's screen was invented to fix one particular problem. Knowing which problem tells you where each tool is still weak.

Feb 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Before the chart

For most of history, the schedule for a big job lived in the master builder's head and in the contracts with the trades. It worked when one person could hold the whole job in mind. Industrial projects, with thousands of parts and hundreds of people, broke that.

Bar charts: Adamiecki and Gantt

In 1896 a Polish engineer, Karol Adamiecki, drew what he called a harmonogram: tasks as bars against a time scale, used to coordinate work in steel mills. He published it in Polish, decades later, and it was barely noticed outside Poland.

In the 1910s the American engineer Henry Gantt drew similar charts for factory production, and the United States Army used them for munitions and shipbuilding during the First World War. His name stuck. The bar chart is still the most widely read picture in project management, because anyone can read it in five seconds.

What it fixed: a shared picture of who does what, when. What it left: no logic. A bar chart shows dates, not why. Move one bar and nothing else moves. See Every Date Needs a Because.

Line of balance

In the early 1940s, Goodyear developed the line of balance to track production of repeated units, and the US Navy adopted it for production programmes. Plotted as one line per crew across floors or units over time, it shows each crew's rate and where one is about to run into the next. It suits towers, pipelines and housing, where the same work repeats. See The First Floor Is the Slowest.

The critical path method, 1957

In 1957, James Kelley of Remington Rand and Morgan Walker of DuPont worked out a way to schedule plant construction and maintenance shutdowns on one of the early commercial computers. They modelled the job as activities joined by logic: this has to finish before that can start. The longest chain through the network set the finish date. That chain was the critical path, and every other activity had float.

They also used it to trade time against cost: which activities to speed up, and for how much, to finish earlier at the lowest extra cost.

What it fixed: dates that follow from logic, and a clear answer to which work drives the finish. What it left: fixed durations, and only one kind of constraint. Resources, permits and weather sit outside the logic. See Everything Is a Constraint and Float Is Only as Good as the Logic.

PERT, 1958

At almost the same time, the US Navy's Special Projects Office was building the Polaris submarine missile, with thousands of contractors and work nobody had done before. With Lockheed and the consultants Booz Allen Hamilton, it developed the Program Evaluation and Review Technique.

PERT asked for three estimates for each activity: optimistic, most likely and pessimistic. It combined them into an expected duration, the familiar (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) ÷ 6, and a spread. Add them up along the critical path and you get a probability of hitting a date.

It had a flaw that took a few years to pin down. PERT looked at one path. On a real network, several paths merge into the finish, and the finish waits for whichever is latest. With uncertainty on every path, the chance that at least one runs late is higher than the chance for any single path. PERT systematically promised dates that were too early. This is called merge bias, and it is still in every schedule that adds up single-point durations.

The finish waits for the latest of all the paths, not just the critical one. Single-path arithmetic always looks too good.

Precedence diagrams and lags

Early CPM drew activities as arrows, which needed dummy activities to express some logic and was awkward to draw by hand. In 1961 John Fondahl at Stanford published a way to draw activities as boxes joined by lines, aimed at construction people without computers. Through the 1960s that grew into the precedence diagramming method used today, with start-to-start and finish-to-finish links and lags.

What it fixed: overlapping work, drawn simply. What it left: lags, which hide their reasons. See Lags Hide Reasons.

Earned value

In 1967 the US Department of Defense set out cost and schedule control criteria for its contractors that tied cost, schedule and scope together: planned value, earned value and actual cost. Schedule and cost performance indices came out of that, and so did most monthly project reporting since. They tell you how work done compares with the plan. They don't tell you how sure anyone is about the finish. See How Sure Are We About the Finish?.

PMI and a common language

The Project Management Institute was founded in 1969 by a small group of practitioners who wanted project management treated as a profession. In 1984 it offered the first Project Management Professional certification. In 1987 it published a body of knowledge, and in 1996 the first edition of the PMBOK Guide.

The biggest contribution was a shared vocabulary. Critical path, float, earned value, baseline, change control: a scheduler in Toronto and one in Houston now mean the same things by the same words. Later editions moved from a catalogue of processes toward principles, and added agile and adaptive approaches alongside predictive scheduling. If you are studying for the PMP, the Bootcamp covers it.

Software on every desk

Through the 1960s and 1970s, CPM ran on mainframes and schedulers were specialists. Personal computers changed that. Primavera Project Planner appeared in 1983 and Microsoft Project in 1984, and within a decade every project manager could build a network schedule.

What it fixed: access. What it left: schedules with tens of thousands of activities that only their authors understand, and a whiteboard on site that runs the real job. See The Whiteboard and the Schedule Drift Apart.

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation came from the 1940s, when scientists at Los Alamos used repeated random sampling to solve problems too complex to calculate directly. Applied to a schedule, it samples a duration for every activity from its range, calculates the finish, and does that thousands of times. The result is a distribution of finish dates: a P50, a P80, a P90.

Researchers showed in the 1960s that simulation fixed PERT's merge bias, because every path gets its own random durations in every run. It needed computing power that only became cheap on PCs, and dedicated tools spread through the 1990s and 2000s. Today owners of large projects often ask for a schedule risk analysis before they commit to a date.

Used well, it tells you which risks cost days and how often each path turns critical. See Price Risks in Days, Not Colours and The Critical Path Moves. Used badly, it is run once for the owner and filed.

The Last Planner System

In the 1990s, Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell, working on lean construction, noticed that a large share of the work planned for each week didn't get done, because something it needed wasn't ready. Their Last Planner System put planning in the hands of the foremen doing the work, screened tasks for constraints before committing to them, and measured the percentage of weekly promises kept.

What it fixed: plans the field believes, and constraints cleared before the work is scheduled. See Keep Two Weeks of Ready Work and Which Constraint Do We Clear Today?.

Advanced Work Packaging

On the large industrial projects of the 2000s, especially in Alberta's oil sands, owners found that crews spent a large part of each day waiting for material, drawings, tools, scaffold or permits. Workface planning, developed by owners there, and research by the Construction Industry Institute in the early 2010s became Advanced Work Packaging, or AWP.

AWP plans the job from the path of construction backwards. The site is split into construction work areas. Engineering and procurement deliver in the order construction needs. Work reaches crews as installation work packages, each one about a week of work for one crew, released only when everything it needs is in place: drawings, material, scaffold, permits, access.

What it fixed: work released only when it can be finished, and engineering sequenced to suit the field. What it left: a lot of planning effort, which pays off most on big, repetitive industrial jobs. See Count the Handoffs and Measure Time to Tools.

The pattern

  • Bar charts made the plan readable, without logic.
  • CPM added logic, with fixed durations and one kind of constraint.
  • PERT added uncertainty, and promised dates that were too early.
  • Monte Carlo fixed the arithmetic, and is often run once and filed.
  • Last Planner and AWP brought in the constraints that stop real work, mostly outside the main schedule.

The next step is joining them: one plan where logic, resources, permits, weather and decisions are all conditions on the work, uncertainty is simulated across all of them, and the foreman's week and the owner's finish date come from the same model. See Planning From Conditions: The Long Version.
