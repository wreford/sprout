A Short History of Scheduling, From Gantt to Monte Carlo

Every scheduling tool was invented to fix one problem, and each one left a different problem behind. Knowing which is the most useful thing to know about the tools you use.

Bar charts

Karol Adamiecki, a Polish engineer, drew task bars against a time scale in 1896 to coordinate work in steel mills. He published it in Polish decades later and almost nobody outside Poland noticed. Henry Gantt drew similar charts in the 1910s, the U.S. Army used them in the First World War, and his name stuck.

A bar chart is still the easiest schedule to read. It shows dates but not why. Move one bar and nothing else moves.

Line of balance

In the early 1940s Goodyear developed the line of balance for repeated units: one line per crew across floors or units over time, showing each crew's rate and where one is about to run into the next. It suits towers, pipelines and housing, and it's still underused in construction.

Critical path, 1957

James Kelley of Remington Rand and Morgan Walker of DuPont modelled plant construction and shutdowns as activities joined by logic, on one of the early commercial computers. The longest chain set the finish date, and everything else had float. They also used it to work out which activities to speed up, for the least extra cost, to finish earlier.

CPM gave dates a reason. It also fixed the durations and treated logic as the only constraint, so permits, cranes and weather still sit outside the model today.

PERT, 1958

The U.S. Navy, building the Polaris missile with Lockheed and Booz Allen Hamilton, asked for three estimates per activity: optimistic, most likely and pessimistic. PERT combined them, (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) ÷ 6, and added them up along the critical path to get the chance of hitting a date.

The problem took a few years to pin down. Several paths merge into the finish, and the finish waits for whichever is latest. Looking at one path makes the date look better than it is. PERT's dates came out too early, every time, and so does any schedule that adds up single-point durations. It's called merge bias.

Precedence diagrams and lags

In 1961 John Fondahl at Stanford published a way to draw activities as boxes joined by lines, aimed at construction people without computers. It grew into the precedence method used today, with start-to-start and finish-to-finish links and lags. Overlapping work became easy to draw. The lags hide their reasons.

Earned value, PMI and the PC

In 1967 the U.S. Department of Defense set rules tying cost, schedule and scope together, which gave us planned value, earned value, SPI and CPI. They tell you how work done compares with the plan. They don't tell you how sure anyone is about the finish.

PMI was founded in 1969, offered the first PMP certification in 1984 and published the first PMBOK Guide in 1996. Its biggest contribution was a shared vocabulary. Primavera (1983) and Microsoft Project (1984) put network schedules on every desk, which also produced schedules with tens of thousands of activities that only their authors understand. If you're studying for the PMP, the Bootcamp covers the exam.

Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo simulation samples a duration for every activity, calculates the finish, and repeats that thousands of times. The result is a range of finish dates instead of one. It fixes merge bias, because every path gets its own random durations in every run. It needed cheap computing, so it spread in the 1990s and 2000s. Too often it gets run once for the owner and filed.

Last Planner and AWP

In the 1990s Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell noticed that a big share of each week's planned work didn't get done, because something it needed wasn't ready. The Last Planner System put weekly planning in the foremen's hands, screened work for constraints before committing to it, and measured how many weekly promises were kept.

Advanced Work Packaging came from the same problem on big industrial jobs, especially in Alberta's oil sands, where crews spent much of the day waiting for material, drawings, scaffold or permits. Workface planning and Construction Industry Institute research in the early 2010s turned into AWP: plan from the path of construction backwards, have engineering and procurement deliver in that order, and release work to crews only when everything they need is in place.

Both brought the things that stop work back into planning, mostly outside the main schedule. The next step is one plan where logic, resources, permits, weather and decisions are all constraints, uncertainty is simulated across all of them, and the foreman's week and the owner's finish date come from the same model.
