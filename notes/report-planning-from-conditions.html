Planning From Conditions: The Long Version

Most schedules are built from activities and logic ties. Most work on site is stopped by something else: a permit, a crane, a missing part, the weather. Here is what changes when you plan from the conditions instead.

May 24, 2026 · 3 min read

The problem with logic ties

The critical path method was developed in the late 1950s, when computing time was scarce. It models a project as activities joined by logic: this finishes before that starts. It was a huge step forward, and it is still the backbone of every serious schedule.

Ask a superintendent what stopped work this week, though, and the answer is rarely a predecessor. It's a permit that wasn't signed, a crane booked elsewhere, a delivery that didn't come, wind over the lift limit, a room too crowded to work in, an inspector who was away. Those live in other places: the permit log, the crane sheet, the procurement tracker, the superintendent's head. The schedule finds out late. See Everything Is a Constraint.

One kind of thing

All of those are the same kind of thing: a condition that has to be true before work can start. A logic tie is one kind of condition. A permit is another. So is crane availability, a weather window, a signed hold point, an owner decision, a qualified welder being on site.

Each condition can be described with the same few fields:

  • what work it holds up;
  • what makes it true, or clears it;
  • who owns clearing it;
  • when it expires, if it does.

Write conditions down that way, in one place, and several useful things follow.

What you get

Dates with reasons. Every start date points at the conditions holding it: the pour is Thursday because the inspection is Wednesday and the pump is free Thursday. When a condition changes, you can see which dates move. See Every Date Needs a Because and Lags Hide Reasons.

Open items that explain themselves. Follow any blocked task through its conditions until you reach a person and a date. See Ask "Waiting on What?" Until You Hit a Name.

A morning list. Rank conditions by how much work clearing each one would release, and clear the top ones first. See Which Constraint Do We Clear Today?.

Ready work you can count. Work is ready when all its conditions are true at once. Count ready crew-days ahead of each crew, and you see idle time coming before it arrives. See Keep Two Weeks of Ready Work.

Shared capacity in the plan. A crane, a room, a road or an outage window is a condition two activities can't hold at once. See The Crane Is a Predecessor and Merge the Contractors' Plans.

Rules the plan is checked against. Site rules become checks that run every time the plan changes, and some plans fail them and don't get published. See The Schedule Doesn't Know the Site Rules.

Expiries that warn you. Permits, tags and certifications that lapse mid-activity get flagged while there's time to renew. See Everything on Site Expires.

Fast what-ifs. Change one condition and see what moves, while everyone is still in the room. See Ask What-If Before the Meeting Ends.

Risk in days. Put uncertainty on the conditions and simulate, and each risk gets a price in days. See Price Risks in Days, Not Colours.

The schedule becomes what the conditions produce, instead of a picture someone keeps up to date by hand.

What it costs

It is more data than a plain schedule, and someone has to keep it current. The fix is to capture each condition once, where the work happens: on the timesheet, in the foreman's diary, at the hold point. See Stop Retyping the Foreman's Diary and the timesheet sticky.

It also needs judgment about how much to model. Not every bolt needs its own condition. Start with the conditions behind your last few delays.

How to start

  1. Take next week's lookahead.
  2. For every activity, list what has to be true before it can start, in plain words.
  3. Give each condition an owner and, where it has one, an expiry.
  4. Every morning, ask which conditions to clear to free the most work.
  5. After a month, look at which kinds of condition held you up most. Those are the ones worth modelling properly.

A spreadsheet is enough to start. The habit matters more than the tool.
