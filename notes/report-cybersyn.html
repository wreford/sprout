Stafford Beer and Project Cybersyn

In 1971 a British management consultant went to Chile to help run a national economy from daily reports sent over telex machines. It only partly worked, but the ideas behind it apply to any big project.

Who he was

Stafford Beer was a British management consultant and operational researcher. In the 1950s and 1960s he worked in the steel industry and then ran his own consulting firm. He spent his career on one question: how does a large organization notice its own problems and fix them fast enough?

He called his answer the Viable System Model. The short version: an organization survives when the people doing the work can handle most problems themselves, and only the problems they can't handle go up the chain, quickly. Head office shouldn't be making decisions the site can make. But when the site is in trouble, head office needs to know right away, not at the end of the month.

The call from Chile

In 1970 Salvador Allende was elected president of Chile. His government nationalized a large part of the economy: mines, factories, banks. Suddenly the state was responsible for hundreds of businesses, with few managers and no good way of knowing what was happening in them.

In 1971 Fernando Flores, a young engineer at the state development agency, wrote to Beer and asked for help. Beer agreed, and the project that came out of it was called Cybersyn.

What they built

Cybersyn had four parts:

  • Cybernet: a network of telex machines, many of them found sitting unused in a government warehouse, connecting factories to a central office in Santiago. Each day, factories sent in a small set of numbers: production, raw materials, absenteeism and so on.
  • Cyberstride: computer software that took those daily numbers and looked for anything moving outside its normal range, so trouble showed up early.
  • CHECO: a simulator meant to model the national economy and test decisions before making them. It never got far.
  • The operations room: a hexagonal room in Santiago with seven swivel chairs, buttons in the armrests and screens on the walls, where managers would see the state of the economy and make decisions together.

One part of the design matters most for project people. Beer built in what he called algedonic signals: if a problem at a factory wasn't dealt with locally within a set time, the alert went up a level automatically, and kept going up until someone dealt with it. Nobody could sit on bad news.

The one real test

In October 1972, truck owners across Chile went on strike, and much of the country's transport stopped. The government used the telex network to find out where food and fuel were short and which roads were open, and to direct the trucks that were still running. Supplies kept moving. It was the clearest example of the system doing what it was built for: getting accurate news from the field to the people who could act on it, the same day.

How it ended

The operations room was finished as a prototype in 1972. It looked impressive and photographed well, but it never worked as the real decision room it was meant to be. The data coming in was often late or rough, the software was still being developed, and the government was dealing with a political and economic crisis.

On September 11, 1973, the military overthrew Allende's government. Cybersyn was shut down and the operations room was dismantled. Beer continued working and writing until his death in 2002.

What a project manager can take from it

  • A few numbers, every day, from the field. Cybersyn didn't try to collect everything. It collected a small set of numbers daily and watched for changes. See Measure How Long Crews Wait Before Starting.
  • Put a timer on bad news. The algedonic signal is the best idea in the project. If a problem isn't handled at the level where it started within a set time, it goes up automatically. See Measure How Long Bad News Takes to Reach the Schedule and Follow Every Open Item to a Name and a Date.
  • Let the site handle what it can. Beer's model only sends up what the level below can't deal with. That matches how good superintendents already work. See Clear the Constraint That Frees the Most Work.
  • The room is the easy part. The operations room was the part everyone remembers, and the part that mattered least. Clean daily data and people willing to act on it were the hard parts. See the sticky note on good-looking Gantt charts.
