Stafford Beer and Project Cybersyn

In 1971 a British consultant went to Chile to help run the country's nationalized factories from daily telex reports. The control room is what people remember. The useful idea was a timer on bad news.

Stafford Beer was a British management consultant who spent his career on one question: how does a big organization notice its own problems and fix them fast enough? His answer was that the people doing the work should handle most problems themselves, and only what they can't handle should go up the chain, quickly.

In 1970 Salvador Allende's government in Chile nationalized mines, factories and banks. The state suddenly ran hundreds of businesses with few managers and no good way of knowing what was happening in them. In 1971 Fernando Flores, a young engineer at the state development agency, asked Beer for help. The project was called Cybersyn.

What they built

Factories sent a few numbers a day over a network of telex machines, many of them found unused in a government warehouse: production, raw materials, absenteeism. Software in Santiago looked for anything moving outside its normal range. There was also an economic simulator that never got far, and an operations room: six-sided, with seven swivel chairs, buttons in the armrests and screens on the walls.

The timer

The part worth copying was simple. If a factory didn't fix a problem within a set time, the alert went up a level automatically, and kept going up until someone dealt with it. Nobody could sit on bad news.

The system got one real test. In October 1972, truck owners across Chile went on strike. The government used the telex network to find out where food and fuel were short and which roads were open, and to direct the trucks that were still running. Supplies kept moving.

The room

The operations room was finished as a prototype in 1972. It photographed well and it's the image everyone remembers, but it never worked as a real decision room. The data was often late or rough and the software was still being built. On September 11, 1973, the military overthrew Allende's government and the project was shut down.

The timer is easy to copy on a job, and it needs no room or software. Give every open problem a deadline at the level where it starts. When the deadline passes, it goes to the next level up, without anyone having to decide to escalate it.
