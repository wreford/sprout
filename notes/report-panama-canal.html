How the Panama Canal Got Built

France spent eight years and thousands of lives and failed. The United States finished it in ten. The difference was mostly planning: health, housing, logistics and a design that fit the ground.

Nov 22, 2026 · 4 min read

The French attempt

In 1881 a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, fresh from building the Suez Canal, started digging across the isthmus of Panama. The plan was a sea-level canal, like Suez: a trench from ocean to ocean with no locks.

Panama was not Suez. Suez crossed flat, dry sand. Panama crossed a mountain ridge, a river that flooded violently in the wet season, and some of the most unstable ground on earth. Every rainy season the Chagres River rose and swamped the works. The deep cut through the continental divide kept sliding back into itself.

Then there was disease. Yellow fever and malaria killed workers by the thousand. Nobody yet accepted that mosquitoes carried both, so the hospitals stood the beds in bowls of water to keep the ants off, and the water bred the mosquitoes. The usual estimate is that over twenty thousand people died in the French years.

By 1889 the company was bankrupt. The work stopped, and the machinery was left to rust in the jungle.

The American start, and a false one

The United States took over in 1904, after backing Panama's separation from Colombia and signing a treaty for a canal zone. The first chief engineer, John Findley Wallace, inherited the French plan, the French equipment and the same diseases. He faced pressure from Washington to show dirt moving, so he dug. Within a year there was a yellow fever outbreak, workers were leaving, and Wallace resigned.

Stevens stops the digging

His replacement in 1905, John Stevens, was a railroad engineer. His first decision was the one that made the canal possible: he slowed the digging almost to a stop.

Instead he built the things that would let digging happen at scale. He rebuilt the Panama Railroad with heavier track and double lines. He built housing, mess halls, stores and clubs for tens of thousands of workers. He paved the streets of Panama City and Colón and gave them running water and sewers. And he gave full backing to the chief sanitary officer, William Gorgas, who had already beaten yellow fever in Havana by attacking mosquitoes.

Gorgas's teams drained and oiled standing water, screened buildings and fumigated houses across the zone. Yellow fever was gone from the zone by the end of 1906. Malaria was cut sharply, though never eliminated.

The first critical path on the canal was not the canal. It was the railroad, the water supply and the mosquitoes.

Locks, a lake and a dam

Stevens also argued for changing the design. A sea-level canal meant digging far deeper through the divide and taming the Chagres. A lock canal could lift ships up to a lake and down again. In 1906 the United States chose locks.

The plan was elegant. An earth dam at Gatun would hold back the Chagres and make Gatun Lake, at the time the largest artificial lake in the world. Ships would climb three steps of locks from the Atlantic to the lake, cross it, pass through the cut in the divide, and descend through two more sets of locks to the Pacific. The river that had wrecked the French became the canal's water supply.

Goethals and the cut

Stevens resigned in 1907. President Roosevelt handed the job to an Army engineer, George Washington Goethals, so that the chief engineer could not simply walk away. Goethals ran the job until it was finished, with tight central control and a Sunday court where any worker could bring a complaint to him directly.

The hardest work was the Culebra Cut, the passage through the continental divide. Steam shovels loaded spoil trains on tracks that were shifted continually as the cut deepened. Stevens's railroad system moved the spoil out to build the Gatun Dam and breakwaters. Slides kept pouring back into the cut, sometimes burying shovels and track, and the crews dug the same ground again. The cut ended up much wider than designed because the slopes would not stand any steeper.

Who did the work

Most of the labour came from the Caribbean, especially Barbados. Workers were paid on two rolls: the gold roll, mostly white Americans paid in gold, and the silver roll, mostly Black West Indian workers paid less in silver, housed worse and given worse care. The silver roll did the most dangerous work and suffered most of the deaths. Thousands of workers died in the American years too.

Opening

The locks were poured in concrete on a scale nobody had attempted, with steel gates and electric towing locomotives to guide ships through. The canal opened in August 1914, ahead of the schedule Goethals had set, as war began in Europe.

What a project manager can take from it

  • Logistics, health and housing are the first critical path on any remote job, not overhead. Stevens built the system before he used it. See Build the Railroad Before You Dig.
  • Pressure to show progress can be the most expensive thing on the job. Wallace dug because Washington wanted dirt moving. It cost a year.
  • Fit the design to the ground. The lock design turned the flooding river from the main threat into the water supply.
  • Some constraints are physical and will not negotiate. The slides set the width of the cut, whatever the drawings said. See the Hoover Dam cooling pipes for another.
  • Who carries the risk matters. The silver roll carried the danger and got the least for it. Every big job still has a version of that question.
