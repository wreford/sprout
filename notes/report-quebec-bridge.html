The Quebec Bridge: Two Collapses and What They Taught

The longest cantilever span in the world fell twice while it was being built, killing 88 workers. The first collapse is still taught as a case of a warning that couldn't travel fast enough.

Oct 4, 2026 · 3 min read

The project

At the start of the 1900s, Quebec City wanted a bridge across the St. Lawrence. The river is wide and deep at Quebec, with strong tides and winter ice, so the design was a cantilever: two huge steel arms reaching out from each shore, joined in the middle by a suspended span.

The Phoenix Bridge Company of Pennsylvania designed and built it. The most respected bridge engineer in America, Theodore Cooper, was hired as consulting engineer. He was elderly, in poor health, and worked from New York. He visited the site only a handful of times.

A longer span, the same numbers

Cooper lengthened the main span to 1,800 feet (549 metres), which made it the longest cantilever span in the world. Lengthening a span adds steel, and steel adds weight. The weights used to check the design were the estimates from before the change, and they were never properly redone. By the time the error was found, much of the steel was already made and erected. The real dead load was far higher than the design had assumed.

The members were carrying more than they had been designed for from the day they went up.

The warning

In August 1907, the inspector on site, Norman McLure, noticed that some of the main compression members in the south arm were bending. He measured them, and the bends got worse. He reported to Cooper by letter, and the engineers argued over whether the bends had been in the steel from the start.

McLure went to New York to see Cooper in person. Cooper sent a telegram to the Phoenix company telling them to add no more load to the bridge until the matter was looked into. It was not acted on at the site in time. That afternoon, 29 August 1907, the south arm collapsed into the river. Seventy-five workers were killed. Many were Mohawk ironworkers from Kahnawake.

The inquiry

A Royal Commission found that the compression members were not strong enough for the loads, that the dead load had been badly underestimated, and that the professional judgement of Cooper and of the Phoenix company's chief design engineer had failed. It also found that nobody on site had the authority, or the confidence, to stop the work on their own. The people who could see the problem could not stop it, and the person who could stop it could not see it.

The second collapse

The bridge was redesigned, much heavier, and building started again. In September 1916, as the centre span was being lifted into place from barges, a support casting failed and the span fell into the river. Thirteen workers died. A new span was built and lifted successfully the following year, and the bridge carried its first train at the end of 1917. It is still the longest cantilever bridge span in the world.

The iron ring

Many Canadian engineers wear an iron ring on the little finger of their working hand, given at a ceremony when they graduate. The story often told is that the first rings were made from the steel of the fallen bridge. That isn't true. The point of the story is: engineers carry a reminder that their mistakes kill people.

What a project manager can take from it

  • Check the assumptions when the design changes. The span grew and the loads stayed the same on paper. See Assumptions Expire.
  • The person who sees the danger must be able to stop the work. A stop order that has to travel to another city and back is too slow. See How Long Bad News Takes to Reach the Plan.
  • An expert at a distance is not supervision. Cooper's reputation stood in for presence on site.
  • Heavy lifts get a second look, every time. The second collapse happened at the moment of greatest risk, on a job that had already been through a disaster.
