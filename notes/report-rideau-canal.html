The Rideau Canal: Building Through the Swamp

In six years, with hand tools, a British army engineer and a few thousand labourers built a navigable route from Ottawa to Kingston. It cost several times its estimate, it cost hundreds of lives, and it is still in use.

Mar 1, 2026 · 3 min read

Why it was built

After the War of 1812, the British worried that the St. Lawrence River, the main supply route into Upper Canada, ran right along the American border. A route inland, from the Ottawa River to Lake Ontario at Kingston, would let boats move troops and supplies without passing American guns.

In 1826 Lieutenant-Colonel John By of the Royal Engineers was sent to build it. The route ran through about 200 kilometres of forest, rock, rapids and swamp, with almost no roads and no towns along most of it. The town that grew around his headquarters at the north end was called Bytown. It is now Ottawa.

The design: drown the rapids

The obvious way to build a canal is to dig a channel and line it with locks. By had neither the time nor the money to dig 200 kilometres through the Canadian Shield. So he did something cleverer: he built dams to raise the water level and flood the rapids, turning the rivers and lakes along the route into a chain of still-water reaches. Locks were built at the dams to lift boats from one reach to the next.

This slackwater design meant far less excavation. It also meant some of the largest dams in North America at the time, including the high stone arch dam at Jones Falls, built by masons who had never seen anything like it.

He didn't dig through the land. He raised the water until the land didn't matter.

The workforce and the fever

The work was done by contractors with crews of Irish immigrants and French-Canadian labourers, with Scottish stonemasons and Royal Sappers and Miners for the skilled work. Most of it was done by hand: picks, shovels, black powder and oxen.

Malaria was endemic in the swamps of eastern Ontario at the time, and it hit the workforce hard every summer. Hundreds of workers died, along with family members living in the camps. Many are buried in unmarked graves along the canal. Accidents with blasting and falling rock killed more.

Cost and recall

The first estimates were far too low. By kept building to a standard he thought the job needed, with bigger locks than first planned so that steamboats could use the canal. The final cost was several times the original estimate.

The canal opened in May 1832, and By travelled its length by steamboat. He was then recalled to England to answer for the overspending. He was never formally censured, and never given credit either. He died a few years later, his reputation damaged.

What happened to it

The canal was never needed for war. It became a commercial route for a few decades, then a recreational one. It is the oldest continuously operated canal in North America, many of the locks are still worked by hand, and it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2007.

What a project manager can take from it

  • Change the design to fit the constraint. By couldn't afford to dig, so he flooded. See Hoover Dam and the Panama Canal for the same move.
  • Workforce health is on the critical path. The fever came every summer, and nobody could stop it. See Panama: Build the Railroad Before You Dig.
  • Write the assumption beside the estimate. By's bigger locks were a scope change nobody had priced, and he paid for it. See Write the Assumption Next to the Estimate.
  • Build for longer than the brief. The extra size By paid for is why the canal was still useful when its military purpose disappeared.
